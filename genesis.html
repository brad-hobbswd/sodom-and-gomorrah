PART TWO

THE NIGHT THAT BECAME HISTORY

Genesis 19 is the chapter most people remember when they hear the name Sodom. It is also one of the most discussed passages in the biblical tradition. The chapter contains hospitality, fear, violence, sexual aggression, divine judgment, rescue, and the destruction of a city.

Because the chapter has been interpreted in so many different ways, we need to begin with what the narrative actually describes. We should distinguish the actions in the story from later arguments about what those actions mean for modern categories of identity.

CHAPTER 4

The Visitors Arrive

Genesis 19 begins with two visitors arriving at Sodom.

The narrative identifies them as heavenly beings. They have come to the city as part of the larger story that began with Abraham's encounter in Genesis 18.

Lot sees them and welcomes them into his home. He provides food and shelter and urges them to stay under his protection rather than spend the night in the city square.

This is where ancient hospitality becomes central to the story. The visitors are strangers. Lot has taken them into his household, and by doing so he assumes responsibility for their safety.

The peaceful evening does not last.

The men of the city gather outside Lot's house. Genesis describes the gathering as a collective action involving men from the city. They demand that Lot bring the visitors outside.

Their demand is sexual.

The Hebrew narrative describes an attempted assault against the male visitors. The circumstances make the threat unmistakably violent. The crowd is not asking for friendship, conversation, or a consensual relationship. They are demanding access to vulnerable strangers whom Lot has taken into his home.

The event is therefore not presented as a romantic relationship. It is not presented as two people expressing mutual affection. It is an attempted act of sexual violence.

That distinction matters historically because the modern concept of sexual orientation did not exist in the same form in the ancient world. Genesis 19 describes an action. It does not create a modern identity category.

At the same time, we should not use that historical distinction to pretend that the sexual element is absent from the story. It is plainly part of the narrative. The historical task is to describe it accurately without forcing the ancient text into categories it was not using.

The scene also shows why the background of Part One matters. Lot's guests are strangers. He has brought them into his household. The crowd outside is threatening people who are under the protection of his home.

CHAPTER 5

Sex, Violence, and Power

The events outside Lot's house involve more than sexuality alone. They also involve power.

The ancient world frequently connected sexuality with power, domination, honor, status, and humiliation in ways that do not always fit modern categories.

Sexual violence could be used as an expression of domination. An assault could communicate that the victim was powerless and that the aggressor controlled the situation.

That does not make the behavior acceptable. Quite the opposite. Genesis portrays the actions of the crowd as wicked and dangerous.

The men are not portrayed as loving partners. They are portrayed as an aggressive mob threatening strangers under Lot's protection.

This means that Genesis 19 contains several elements simultaneously:

  • sexual violence
  • violence against strangers
  • mob behavior
  • abuse of power
  • rejection of hospitality
  • threats against a household
  • rebellion against God's moral order

The historical reader should therefore resist reducing the entire episode to one modern category.

It is equally important to resist the opposite mistake: claiming that the sexual dimension is irrelevant because the event is violent. The narrative itself includes the sexual demand. Violence and sexuality are intertwined in the scene.

Genesis also shows how quickly a violation of hospitality becomes a wider confrontation. Lot attempts to protect the people inside his home, while the crowd outside refuses to recognize the boundaries of his household.

The response of the crowd shows that the problem is not simply a disagreement about Lot's guests. The men are willing to use intimidation and violence against anyone who stands between them and their demands.

This is one reason the story can be read as a narrative about a society in serious moral collapse. Sexual aggression is present, but so are violence, humiliation, hostility toward strangers, and contempt for another person's responsibility to protect those under his care.

CHAPTER 6

The Judgment of Sodom

The visitors warn Lot that judgment is coming. Lot is told to gather his household and leave the city.

The destruction that follows becomes one of the most famous judgment scenes in the Bible. Sodom and Gomorrah are destroyed, and the surrounding region becomes part of the enduring memory of divine judgment.

Within the narrative, this is not presented as a random event. It is presented as divine judgment against a society whose wickedness has become grave.

Lot and those with him are given an opportunity to escape. The story therefore contains both destruction and deliverance.

Genesis 19 should not be separated from the chapters that came before it. Genesis 13 had already described Sodom as wicked. Genesis 18 had described a great outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah. Genesis 19 then gives the reader a disturbing example of the corruption that exists within the city.

Later biblical writers will look back at this event and emphasize different aspects of Sodom's wickedness. Ezekiel will emphasize pride, abundance, and failure to help the poor and needy. Jude will connect Sodom with sexual immorality and the pursuit of “different flesh.” Jesus will use Sodom when speaking about towns that reject his messengers. Peter will remember Sodom in connection with judgment, lawlessness, and corrupt desire.

These later references do not erase Genesis 19. They become part of the continuing history of how Sodom was remembered.

WHAT GENESIS HAS SHOWN US

Genesis presents Sodom as a wicked society before its destruction. Genesis 19 then gives the reader a specific and disturbing example of that wickedness: an aggressive crowd attempts sexual violence against male visitors who have entered Lot's household.

The episode includes sexuality, but it also includes violence, abuse of power, hostility toward strangers, rejection of hospitality, and rebellion against God.

Genesis gives us the beginning of the story. The next question is how later biblical writers remembered Sodom and what they believed its destruction revealed about the city's deeper corruption.
