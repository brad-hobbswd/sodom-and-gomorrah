PART THREE

HOW ISRAEL REMEMBERED SODOM

The story of Sodom did not end with Genesis. As Israel's history continued, later writers looked back at Sodom and used the city as a warning for their own generation. The memory of Sodom developed within changing historical circumstances that included wars, kings, political division, conquest, and exile.

This is important because the later biblical references do not simply repeat Genesis 19. They remember Sodom from different perspectives and emphasize different aspects of the city's corruption. Ezekiel provides one of the clearest examples.

CHAPTER 6

Ezekiel and the Memory of Sodom

“Behold, this was the iniquity of thy sister Sodom, pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness was in her and in her daughters, neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. And they were haughty, and committed abomination before me: therefore I took them away as I saw good.”

Ezekiel 16:49–50

WHAT THIS PASSAGE REVEALS

Centuries passed. Israel experienced wars, kings, political division, conquest, and eventually exile. During the Babylonian exile, the prophet Ezekiel looked back toward Sodom.

This is one of the most important moments in the history of the Sodom tradition. Ezekiel does not retell Genesis 19. Instead, he uses Sodom as a comparison while speaking about Jerusalem and its own moral condition.

Ezekiel describes Sodom as proud, wealthy, comfortable, and unwilling to help the poor and needy. The passage names these failures directly, so they should shape our reading before we move into the historical explanation.

Ezekiel's description reveals how the memory of Sodom was being used in a later biblical setting. The city was not recalled only in connection with the events narrated in Genesis 19. It had also become a moral example through which a prophet could confront the failures of another society.

In Ezekiel's account, abundance is not presented as evidence of righteousness. Sodom had food and comfort, yet its prosperity did not lead to generosity or care for people in need. Instead, the passage connects material security with pride, arrogance, and a refusal to strengthen the hands of the poor and needy.

The contrast is sharp: wealth had not become compassion, comfort had not become responsibility, and abundance had not produced humility. The problem was not simply that the city possessed resources, but that its people failed to use their strength to support the vulnerable.

This is why Ezekiel's reference matters for understanding the biblical tradition about Sodom. The prophet draws attention to social and moral corruption alongside his mention of an “abomination.” His emphasis is broader than a single accusation: he presents a society marked by pride, excess, neglect of the needy, and other conduct he condemns.

Ezekiel then mentions an “abomination.” The passage therefore contains several different accusations rather than reducing Sodom's corruption to one single category.

Historically, this is significant because Ezekiel is not replacing Genesis 19. He is adding another perspective. The prophetic tradition has taken the memory of Sodom and applied it to a later society, using the city's reputation as a warning about what happens when prosperity, pride, injustice, and moral corruption become deeply established.

Ezekiel's purpose is therefore not to give us a complete list of everything the people of Sodom ever did. His purpose is to make a moral comparison. Jerusalem must recognize that outward prosperity does not protect a society from judgment.

CHAPTER 7

Sodom and Social Justice

“Learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.”

Isaiah 1:17

HOW THE PROPHETS APPLY THIS PRINCIPLE

The prophets repeatedly emphasized the responsibility of the wealthy and powerful toward the vulnerable. Isaiah's instruction provides a clear example of that prophetic expectation and helps frame the social concerns discussed in this chapter.

Widows, orphans, foreigners, and the poor appear throughout the Hebrew Bible as people whom God's people were expected to protect.

This explains why Ezekiel's description of Sodom focuses so heavily on social responsibility. A society could possess enormous wealth and still be considered morally bankrupt.

For Ezekiel, prosperity without compassion was itself evidence of corruption. Having enough was not the same thing as living rightly.

The important historical point is that Ezekiel places social behavior directly into the memory of Sodom. The city becomes an example of what happens when people have abundance but refuse responsibility for vulnerable people around them.

This does not mean that Ezekiel denies the other accusations associated with Sodom. Instead, he highlights the failures that are most useful for the argument he is making to his own generation.

That distinction matters when we read the Bible historically. A later writer can emphasize one aspect of an earlier story without claiming that the earlier story contained only that one issue.

WHAT EZEKIEL ADDS

Genesis gave us the narrative of Sodom's destruction. Ezekiel gives us another lens: pride, abundance, comfort, failure to help the poor and needy, and other forms of moral corruption.

The memory of Sodom has therefore become broader. It is no longer only the story of what happened on one night. It has become a warning about what can happen to a society when prosperity exists without humility, compassion, and responsibility.
