PART THREE

HOW ISRAEL REMEMBERED SODOM

Genesis 19 brought the reader through the destruction of Sodom and the difficult aftermath experienced by Lot and his daughters. The account closed with the city remembered through violence, judgment, rescue, and loss, while also pointing toward a larger question: how would later generations understand and use the memory of Sodom? The biblical story did not disappear with the smoke that Abraham saw rising from the plain. As Israel's history unfolded, its prophets returned to Sodom as a familiar example through which they could confront the conduct of their own people.

This part of the study turns from the events narrated in Genesis to the way a later prophet interpreted Sodom's reputation. Ezekiel does not retell the events of Genesis 19, nor does he offer a complete catalogue of the city's offenses. Instead, he places Sodom within a prophetic message addressed to Jerusalem, drawing attention to pride, abundance, complacency, and the failure to care for people in need. Reading his words in their immediate setting allows us to see both the continuity with Genesis and the distinctive emphasis Ezekiel brings to the biblical memory of the city.1

CHAPTER 10

Ezekiel and the Memory of Sodom

“Behold, this was the iniquity of your sister Sodom: pride, overabundance of food, prosperous ease, and idleness were hers and her daughters’; neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. And they were haughty and committed abominable offenses before Me; therefore I removed them when I saw it and I saw fit.”

Ezekiel 16:49–50

WHAT THIS PASSAGE REVEALS

The setting of Ezekiel's message is different from the world described in Genesis. The opening of the book places the prophet among Judean exiles in Babylonia and dates his visions in relation to the exile of King Jehoiachin. Ezekiel's ministry therefore belongs to a period of national crisis, when Jerusalem's political future, religious life, and covenant identity were under severe pressure. The prophet speaks to a community living with the consequences of conquest and displacement, and he repeatedly challenges the assumptions that had allowed Jerusalem to regard itself as secure despite its unfaithfulness.2

Ezekiel 16 develops this challenge through an extended allegory in which Jerusalem is portrayed as a woman whose life is traced from abandonment and vulnerability to care, growth, covenant relationship, and eventual unfaithfulness. The chapter is deliberately forceful in its language. Jerusalem's conduct is compared with that of Samaria and Sodom, cities presented as her sisters, and the comparison is intended to expose the depth of Jerusalem's wrongdoing rather than to provide a neutral account of the history of those places. The language of sisterhood is part of the chapter's prophetic imagery, and the comparison should be read within that literary design.3

Within this larger argument, Ezekiel 16:49–50 identifies several features of Sodom's iniquity. The prophet names pride, fullness of food, and prosperous ease, then gives particular attention to the city's refusal to strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. The description presents abundance as a responsibility that Sodom failed to honor. The city had resources, yet its prosperity did not produce generosity or concern for people who lacked the means to meet their needs. Instead, its comfort existed alongside arrogance and neglect, exposing a profound failure in the way its people treated others.4

The wording of the passage deserves careful attention. Ezekiel does not merely say that Sodom possessed wealth; he connects its abundance with pride and with a refusal to assist the poor and needy. The issue is not the possession of food or material security by itself, but the moral condition revealed when those advantages are enjoyed without compassion or responsibility. Prosperity becomes evidence against the city when those who have the ability to help refuse to strengthen those who are vulnerable. In this way, Ezekiel's account moves beyond a description of private behavior and addresses the character of an entire community.

Verse 50 adds that Sodom's people were haughty and committed an abomination before God. Ezekiel does not explain the word “abomination” in this verse by naming a specific act, so readers should be cautious about supplying a precise definition that the immediate wording does not provide. The term appears elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible in a range of religious, ethical, and social contexts, and its meaning must be considered in the passage where it occurs. Here, the prophet places the statement alongside pride, excess, and neglect of the poor, while the wider chapter continues its indictment of Jerusalem's unfaithfulness.5

The verses that follow are also important. Ezekiel 16:51–52 continues the comparison by declaring that Jerusalem's wrongdoing has exceeded that of Samaria and Sodom, leaving Jerusalem without grounds for self-righteousness. The point is not that the three cities had identical histories or committed precisely the same offenses. Ezekiel uses the comparison to overturn Jerusalem's sense of moral superiority. A people who considered themselves better than others are confronted with the possibility that their own conduct has become more reprehensible. Sodom serves as a known example in an argument directed toward Jerusalem itself.6

This is one of the most significant developments in the biblical memory of Sodom. Genesis tells the story of the city's destruction, while Ezekiel recalls the city as a warning about pride, excessive comfort, and the neglect of people in need. The prophet does not cancel the earlier account, and he does not claim that these are the only wrongs associated with Sodom. Rather, he selects the features that serve his message to Jerusalem and applies the memory of judgment to a new generation. The same city can therefore be remembered in more than one way without requiring the biblical passages to be treated as though they were making identical arguments.

CHAPTER 11

Sodom, Prosperity, and Responsibility

“Learn to do right! Seek justice, relieve the oppressed, and correct the oppressor. Defend the fatherless, plead for the widow.”

Isaiah 1:17

HOW THE PROPHETS APPLY THIS PRINCIPLE

Ezekiel's description of Sodom belongs to a wider prophetic concern with the treatment of people who possess little power or protection. Across the Hebrew Bible, Israel is repeatedly instructed to remember the needs of the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the foreigner. These commands are not presented as optional expressions of personal kindness; they are part of the community's responsibility before God. The law calls Israel to provide for the vulnerable, while the prophets challenge those who maintain religious appearances but tolerate exploitation and indifference.7

Isaiah's instruction to seek justice, relieve the oppressed, and defend the fatherless and widow illustrates this prophetic expectation. Isaiah and Ezekiel speak in different historical circumstances and address different audiences, so their messages should not be collapsed into one sermon. Their writings nevertheless share a concern that worship and covenant identity cannot be separated from conduct toward other people. A community's treatment of those who are easily overlooked becomes part of the evidence by which its faithfulness is judged.8

Other prophetic passages make the same concern unmistakable. Amos condemns those who trample the poor and take advantage of the needy, while also calling the people to seek good, reject evil, and establish justice. Micah summarizes the demands of faithful living through the call to do justice, love mercy, and walk humbly with God. These passages do not all refer directly to Sodom, and they should not be presented as additional descriptions of the city. They provide a broader biblical context for understanding why Ezekiel could use the neglect of the poor as a central part of his indictment.9

The connection between prosperity and responsibility is especially important in Ezekiel 16. The prophet does not condemn abundance simply because a community has enough to eat or enjoys security. His criticism concerns the pride and indifference that can grow when comfort becomes an excuse to ignore the needs of others. A society may have the means to strengthen the vulnerable and still choose not to do so. In Ezekiel's account, that refusal reveals the moral character of the community more clearly than its outward wealth can conceal it.

This emphasis also helps readers avoid reducing the prophetic memory of Sodom to a single accusation. Genesis 19 gives a detailed narrative of a threatened assault, hostility toward strangers, and the destruction that follows. Ezekiel 16 addresses Jerusalem through a comparison with Sodom and highlights arrogance, abundance, and neglect of the poor. Each passage has its own purpose, and neither should be forced to carry every part of the other's argument. The differences are not a problem to be explained away; they are part of the way the biblical writers applied a shared memory to distinct moral and theological concerns.

For readers today, Ezekiel's words invite serious reflection on the relationship between resources and responsibility. The passage asks what happens when people or communities have enough to help but become so absorbed in their own comfort that the suffering of others no longer moves them. Its warning is directed first toward Jerusalem within Ezekiel's prophetic message, yet the principle it expresses reaches beyond that historical audience: abundance does not establish righteousness, and religious identity does not excuse indifference. A community's moral life is revealed not only by what it possesses, but also by how it responds to those who need support.

WHAT EZEKIEL ADDS TO THE JOURNEY

Genesis has shown how Sodom's destruction entered the biblical narrative. Ezekiel now shows how that memory was used generations later to confront Jerusalem. His emphasis on pride, abundance, prosperous ease, and the failure to help the poor and needy adds an important dimension to the study, while his comparison with Samaria and Jerusalem reminds readers that prophetic references must be understood within their immediate literary and historical settings.

The next part turns to Leviticus and the covenant laws given to Israel. That transition requires a distinction between a prophetic interpretation of Sodom and legislation addressed to Israel: Ezekiel uses Sodom as a warning, while Leviticus sets out standards for the covenant community. Considering those texts in sequence will help clarify both their points of contact and the different questions each one answers.

Notes and References

The notes below identify the biblical passages discussed in this part and provide selected scholarly resources for readers who wish to explore Ezekiel's historical setting, literary structure, and interpretation in greater depth. Biblical references identify the location of the passages; scholarly works provide interpretive discussion and should be distinguished from the wording of the biblical text itself.

  1. The transition from Genesis to Ezekiel follows the biblical references to Sodom in their canonical sequence. Genesis 19 narrates the destruction, while Ezekiel 16:46–52 recalls Sodom within a prophetic comparison involving Jerusalem and Samaria. ↩
  2. Ezekiel 1:1–3 identifies the prophet among the exiles by the river Chebar and associates his call with the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's captivity. Further chronological notices appear in Ezekiel 8:1; 20:1; 24:1; and 33:21. For historical and literary orientation, see Daniel I. Block, The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 1–24 (Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1997), introduction. ↩
  3. Ezekiel 16:1–63 is an extended allegory concerning Jerusalem. The comparison with Samaria and Sodom appears in verses 44–52, especially verses 46–48. The family language belongs to the chapter's prophetic imagery and should not be mistaken for a literal genealogy or a neutral historical description. See Moshe Greenberg, Ezekiel 1–20, Anchor Bible 22 (Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1983), commentary on Ezekiel 16. ↩
  4. The wording of Ezekiel 16:49 names pride, fullness of food, abundance of prosperous ease, and failure to strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. Compare Deuteronomy 15:7–11, which commands generosity toward the poor, and Deuteronomy 24:17–22, which protects the rights and provision of the foreigner, fatherless, and widow. ↩
  5. Ezekiel 16:50 uses the term “abomination” without specifying the particular act in that verse. The word appears in varied contexts elsewhere, including idolatry and prohibited practices (Deuteronomy 7:25–26; 18:9–12), dishonest conduct (Proverbs 11:1), and other forms of behavior condemned in the Hebrew Bible. The immediate passage should govern interpretation rather than an assumed definition imported from another text. ↩
  6. Ezekiel 16:51–52 extends the comparison by declaring Jerusalem more culpable than Samaria and Sodom. The rhetorical purpose is to expose Jerusalem's wrongdoing and undermine its confidence in its own superiority. See Block, The Book of Ezekiel: Chapters 1–24, and Greenberg, Ezekiel 1–20, on Ezekiel 16:44–52. ↩
  7. The responsibility to protect vulnerable people appears in multiple legal and prophetic texts. Examples include Exodus 22:21–27; Deuteronomy 10:17–19; 14:28–29; 24:17–22; and 27:19. These passages provide a wider covenant context for Ezekiel's criticism of Sodom's neglect of the poor and needy. ↩
  8. Isaiah 1:10–17 criticizes Jerusalem's religious practices when they coexist with injustice and calls the people to seek justice and defend the vulnerable. Isaiah and Ezekiel address different audiences and circumstances; the passage is included here as a related prophetic witness, not as a direct commentary on Ezekiel 16. ↩
  9. Amos 5:11–15 condemns exploitation of the poor and calls for justice; Micah 6:6–8 summarizes the call to act justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with God. Neither passage directly identifies Sodom, but both illuminate the broader prophetic concern with justice and responsibility. ↩
