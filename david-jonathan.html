PART SEVEN

DAVID AND JONATHAN

The New Testament writings examined in the previous part returned to Sodom as a remembered example through which Jesus, Peter, and Jude addressed judgment, accountability, ungodliness, and sexual wrongdoing. Their references showed how later biblical writers could draw upon an earlier account while emphasizing different features of its meaning. That discussion also established an important discipline for the remainder of this study: each passage must be read within its own setting, and conclusions must remain proportionate to the language and evidence available. The next subject requires the same care, although the biblical story now turns away from Sodom and toward a relationship preserved in the books of Samuel.

The account of David and Jonathan is not another interpretation of Sodom, nor does Scripture establish a direct connection between the two stories. Their place in this study is comparative and methodological. The relationship between these men has prompted generations of readers to consider how biblical language describes affection, covenant, loyalty, grief, and intimacy. Because the previous part examined how later writers used an established story for particular purposes, this part considers how readers approach an ancient relationship whose emotional language is unusually vivid and whose precise nature is not defined through modern categories. The subject changes, but the responsibility to listen closely to the text remains.

A helpful companion to this discussion is A Love Older Than Fear, a separate study that examines David and Jonathan through covenant language, Hebrew vocabulary, ancient expressions of affection, grief, translation, and the history of interpretation. Its central invitation is to let the biblical narrative be heard before inherited assumptions determine what readers are willing to notice. The work also recognizes that readers may reach different conclusions about the relationship while still taking the surviving evidence seriously. The present section draws upon those themes as a guide to careful reading, while keeping the historical argument grounded in the biblical passages and the scholarly sources identified in the notes.1

The chapters that follow will first examine the relationship within the political and covenant setting of Saul's reign, then consider the language of affection and grief that gives the story its enduring emotional force. The aim is neither to dismiss the possibility of questions raised by the text nor to claim certainty where the narrative does not provide it. A responsible reading can acknowledge the depth of the bond, distinguish explicit statements from interpretation, and recognize the limits of what the historical record can establish.

CHAPTER 21

The Prince and the Shepherd: Covenant in a Divided Kingdom

“The soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.”

1 Samuel 18:1

The relationship between David and Jonathan begins within a moment of political and military change. David has entered the royal story after defeating Goliath, while Jonathan already occupies a position of importance as the son of King Saul and a commander within Israel's forces. Their backgrounds could hardly be more different. David comes from Bethlehem and has been introduced as a shepherd whose courage and trust in God have brought him into the attention of the king. Jonathan belongs to the royal household and stands within the line of succession. The growing prominence of David could have created rivalry between the two men, particularly in a kingdom where inheritance and political security were closely connected. The narrative, however, introduces their relationship through affection rather than competition.2

First Samuel 18:1 describes Jonathan's soul as being knit to David's soul and states that Jonathan loved him as his own soul. The language is direct and emotionally significant. The narrator does not begin by describing a political alliance or a military arrangement; the account first draws attention to a bond between two people. The following verse records Saul keeping David in the royal household, and verse 3 describes Jonathan making a covenant with David because he loved him as his own soul. The order of these details matters. The relationship is expressed through love, and the covenant gives that attachment a recognizable form of commitment within the narrative.3

The Hebrew word commonly translated “soul” is nephesh. Its range of meaning is broader than the modern idea of an invisible part of a person separated from the body. Depending on context, the word can refer to a living being, a life, a person, or the self. In the statement that Jonathan's nephesh was knit to David's, the expression communicates a profound personal attachment. The wording should not be reduced to a technical description of a particular kind of relationship, but neither should its emotional force be treated as incidental. The narrator has chosen language that presents Jonathan's bond with David as deeply personal and consequential.4

The covenant described in 1 Samuel 18:3 adds another dimension. In the Hebrew Bible, covenant language can describe formal agreements, obligations, loyalty, and enduring commitments between individuals or groups. The precise terms of Jonathan and David's covenant are not fully listed at this point, so readers should be cautious about supplying details the narrator has not provided. What the text does make clear is the reason given for the covenant: Jonathan loved David as his own soul. Their commitment is therefore not presented as a detached political calculation. Personal affection and covenant loyalty appear together at the beginning of the account, and the events that follow show how that commitment shapes Jonathan's choices.5

Verse 4 describes Jonathan removing his robe and giving it to David, along with his garments, sword, bow, and belt. These objects belong to the visible world of status, military identity, and royal privilege. The gesture has often been understood as a symbolic recognition of David's growing importance and future kingship, especially when read alongside the wider narrative of Saul's declining authority. That interpretation is plausible within the political setting, although the passage does not pause to explain every symbolic meaning attached to each object. What can be observed directly is that Jonathan gives David items associated with his own position and equipment. The action reinforces the narrative's portrayal of a prince who does not respond to David's rise with immediate hostility or possessive ambition.6

The political pressure surrounding their relationship becomes more severe as Saul grows increasingly hostile toward David. The king's suspicion turns into repeated attempts to kill him, and Jonathan is placed in a painful position between his father and the man to whom he has pledged loyalty. First Samuel 19 records Jonathan speaking to Saul in David's defense, reminding the king of David's service and asking why he would sin against innocent blood. For a time, Saul listens and swears that David will not be killed. The peace does not last, and the danger soon returns. Jonathan's intervention is significant because his loyalty is expressed through action, even when doing so places him at odds with the king and exposes him to personal risk.7

First Samuel 20 brings the conflict into sharper focus. David seeks Jonathan's help in determining whether Saul intends to kill him, and the two men arrange a way to discover the king's response during the New Moon observance. Their conversation reveals the strain of the situation: David struggles to understand why Saul seeks his life, while Jonathan initially finds the possibility difficult to accept. As events unfold, Jonathan discovers that Saul's anger is not temporary. The king's response makes clear that David remains in mortal danger, and Jonathan must confront the consequences of his father's hostility. The narrative does not portray this as an abstract disagreement about succession. It is a crisis involving family loyalty, covenant obligation, political violence, and the preservation of a life.8

When Jonathan warns David and the two men meet in the field, the story gives space to their grief. First Samuel 20:41 says that they kissed one another and wept together, with David weeping most intensely. Their farewell is followed by a renewed appeal to the covenant they have made before the Lord. The scene is emotionally open, and the text does not treat their tears as a failure of courage or masculinity. Their grief emerges from the danger that has separated them and from the knowledge that their lives may no longer follow the path they once expected. The covenant remains the language through which they express their continuing responsibility toward one another and toward their households.9

The account of David and Jonathan therefore presents a relationship formed within a world of competing claims and increasing danger. Their bond is expressed through love, covenant, protection, gifts, loyalty, and grief. Jonathan's royal position makes his commitment especially consequential because David's rise threatens the future that might ordinarily have been expected for the king's son. Yet the narrative repeatedly shows Jonathan choosing to protect David rather than treat him as a rival. These actions give the relationship its historical and literary depth, and they prepare the reader to consider the language of affection more closely without separating that language from the circumstances in which it appears.

CHAPTER 22

Love, Grief, and the Language of the Text

“I am distressed for thee, my brother Jonathan: very pleasant hast thou been unto me: thy love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women.”

2 Samuel 1:26

The death of Saul and Jonathan in battle brings the relationship to its most painful point. Second Samuel 1 records David receiving news of their deaths and responding with a lament that honors both men. The poem mourns the loss of Israel's fallen leaders, remembers their strength in battle, and expresses David's personal anguish over Jonathan. Within that lament, David calls Jonathan his brother, describes him as deeply pleasant to him, and declares that Jonathan's love was wonderful, surpassing the love of women. The words are among the most emotionally striking in the books of Samuel, and their place within a public lament gives them both personal and communal significance.10

The Hebrew noun often translated “love” in this verse is ahavah, a word used across the Hebrew Bible for forms of affection, attachment, and love. Its meaning is shaped by context rather than by a single modern category. Scripture uses related language for family bonds, friendship, covenant loyalty, desire, and other forms of attachment. The presence of ahavah therefore matters, but the word alone cannot determine whether a relationship should be classified as sexual, romantic, familial, or covenantal. In David's lament, the term communicates the depth and value of Jonathan's love as David experienced and remembered it. A careful reading begins with that direct statement before moving to questions the verse does not answer by itself.11

The comparison with the love of women has attracted particular attention. David does not merely say that Jonathan was a faithful companion or a courageous ally; he compares Jonathan's love with another form of love that was familiar and significant within his world. The wording is unusually strong, and readers are justified in asking why David chose such an intimate comparison within a poem of mourning. Yet the verse does not provide an explicit account of sexual activity between the two men, and it does not use a technical term that settles the relationship's classification. The comparison communicates extraordinary depth, but its precise implications must be considered alongside the whole narrative and the conventions of ancient poetry and lament.12

Ancient expressions of affection between men cannot simply be measured against the emotional conventions of every modern culture. The Hebrew Bible depicts men embracing, kissing, weeping, grieving, making covenants, and expressing loyalty in ways that may feel unfamiliar to readers formed by stricter expectations of masculine restraint. Such gestures could communicate greeting, kinship, reconciliation, farewell, political allegiance, or profound personal affection, depending on the circumstances. Their presence does not automatically establish a sexual relationship, but neither should they be dismissed as meaningless simply because they occur between men. The narrative context and the language surrounding each gesture must guide interpretation.13

The farewell in 1 Samuel 20 and the lament in 2 Samuel 1 belong together in the development of the story. The earlier scene records the two men weeping as they separate under the threat of Saul's violence; the later poem reveals the depth of David's grief after Jonathan has died. The lament does not appear as an isolated emotional statement without a history. It follows years of covenant, danger, protection, separation, and loyalty. David's words are therefore best understood within the accumulated relationship that the books of Samuel have narrated, rather than treated as a single phrase detached from the events that give the poem its meaning.

Readers have approached this relationship from different interpretive positions. Some understand David and Jonathan primarily through the language of covenant friendship and political loyalty. Others believe that the emotional intensity of the narrative, together with David's comparison in 2 Samuel 1:26, permits or invites a romantic reading. Some readers also observe that modern categories of sexual identity do not map neatly onto the social and literary world of ancient Israel. These interpretations should be represented accurately, but none should be confused with a statement explicitly made by the narrator. The biblical text clearly records love, covenant, protection, affection, and grief; it does not explicitly state that David and Jonathan had a sexual relationship.14

That distinction does not require readers to suppress the questions raised by the language. It asks them to separate what the text says from what an interpreter concludes. To declare with certainty that the relationship was sexual would move beyond the explicit evidence. To insist that the language carries no unusual emotional significance would also fail to account for the narrator's repeated emphasis on love, the covenant, the visible grief, and David's extraordinary comparison. The most responsible historical reading acknowledges the strength of the evidence for profound intimacy while remaining honest about what cannot be established from the surviving narrative alone.

The interpretive history surrounding David and Jonathan also deserves attention. Translation choices, cultural expectations, theological commitments, and changing ideas about masculinity have all influenced how readers approach the story. Some traditions have emphasized the political and covenantal dimensions; others have explored the emotional and potentially romantic implications. The existence of disagreement does not mean that every interpretation is equally supported, nor does it allow modern assumptions to replace the ancient text. It does mean that readers should examine the reasons behind their conclusions and recognize when discomfort or inherited expectations may be shaping the way they hear the passage. This is one of the central concerns explored in A Love Older Than Fear, which invites readers to approach the relationship without allowing fear to settle the interpretive question before the biblical language has been considered.15

The story also asks readers to take grief seriously. David's lament is not merely a clue in a debate about the nature of the relationship; it is a poem of loss, admiration, and sorrow. The death of Jonathan removes someone whose loyalty had shaped David's life during a dangerous period, and the language of the lament gives public expression to that loss. The emotional honesty of the poem belongs to the literary world of ancient lament, where grief could be expressed through vivid language and public remembrance. Reading the verse only to settle a modern argument risks overlooking the human experience the poem was written to preserve.

David and Jonathan remain significant because the biblical narrative refuses to reduce their relationship to a single political transaction or a brief expression of admiration. Their story develops through repeated acts of love and loyalty, a covenant made before God, the surrender of symbols associated with royal status, the protection of David from Saul's violence, tears at their separation, and a lament that remembers Jonathan's love as extraordinary. Those elements are explicit in the text. The precise nature of their relationship beyond those descriptions remains a matter of interpretation, and a historically responsible study must preserve that distinction rather than force a certainty the narrative does not provide.

WHAT DAVID AND JONATHAN ADD TO THE JOURNEY

The account of David and Jonathan demonstrates the importance of reading biblical relationships through their language, narrative setting, and historical world. Scripture describes profound affection, covenant commitment, protection, tenderness, and grief between the two men. David's lament gives Jonathan's love extraordinary weight, while the narrative does not explicitly define their relationship in modern sexual categories or state that they had a sexual relationship.

This part has not attempted to turn David and Jonathan into an extension of the Sodom narrative. Their story is a distinct biblical account, included here because the same discipline of careful reading applies: the text must be heard fully, historical context must be respected, and interpretation must not be confused with explicit evidence. The next part returns to the central subject of this study by tracing how Sodom's meaning developed across later generations, bringing the different biblical witnesses examined so far into a broader historical view.
