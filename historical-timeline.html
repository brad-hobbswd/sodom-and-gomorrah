PART EIGHT

HOW THE MEANING OF SODOM DEVELOPED

The biblical memory of Sodom developed as later generations returned to the account of the cities and interpreted its significance within new circumstances. The story did not remain confined to the events narrated in Genesis, nor did every later reference simply repeat the same details. Prophets, teachers, and New Testament writers drew upon the remembered destruction for different purposes, emphasizing particular features of the tradition according to the message they were communicating. Following those references allows us to see how Sodom moved from the name of an ancient city into a powerful moral and theological symbol.

This development should not be mistaken for a series of unrelated meanings invented by later writers. Each reference depends upon the earlier reputation of Sodom and the remembered judgment associated with its destruction. At the same time, the writers do not all emphasize the same offense or draw precisely the same lesson. Their distinct uses of the story are part of the historical evidence, and reading them together requires attention both to their shared foundation and to the particular concerns of each passage.

CHAPTER 23

From City to Symbol

“Behold, this was the iniquity of your sister Sodom: pride, fullness of bread, and prosperous ease was in her and in her daughters. She didn’t strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. They were haughty, and committed abomination before me. Therefore I took them away as I saw good.”

Ezekiel 16:49–50

WHAT THIS PASSAGE REVEALS

One of the clearest examples of this development appears in Ezekiel 16:49–50, where the prophet recalls Sodom while confronting the conduct of Jerusalem. Ezekiel identifies pride, abundant provision, prosperous ease, and the neglect of the poor and needy as features of Sodom's iniquity. His description does not present the city merely as a place remembered for one act of violence in Genesis. Instead, the prophet uses its reputation to expose a pattern of social arrogance in which comfort and abundance existed alongside a refusal to strengthen those who were vulnerable. The comparison becomes especially forceful because Ezekiel directs the accusation toward Jerusalem, a community that should have known the demands of covenant responsibility.

Ezekiel's use of Sodom does not require readers to conclude that the prophet has supplied an exhaustive catalogue of every offense associated with the city. The passage identifies the conditions that matter to his immediate argument. Pride and prosperity had become corrupt when they produced indifference toward the poor and needy, while the prophet also retains the reference to conduct described as an abomination. His emphasis broadens the remembered picture of Sodom without cancelling the other elements preserved elsewhere in the biblical record. The passage is therefore important not because it replaces Genesis, but because it demonstrates how the memory of Sodom could be applied to questions of wealth, responsibility, and injustice.

Isaiah uses the name of Sodom in a different prophetic setting. In Isaiah 1, the prophet addresses Judah and Jerusalem through the language of rebellion, failed worship, and public injustice. He calls the rulers of the people rulers of Sodom and the people themselves people of Gomorrah, using the names as a severe indictment of a community whose religious activity has become disconnected from righteous conduct. The surrounding passage makes the concern unmistakable: offerings and assemblies cannot compensate for hands associated with wrongdoing, and the people are commanded to seek justice, correct oppression, defend the fatherless, and plead for the widow. Sodom functions as a prophetic comparison through which Isaiah exposes the contradiction between religious appearance and unjust behavior.

The use of Sodom in Isaiah and Ezekiel reveals an important feature of the developing tradition. Both prophets draw upon the city's reputation to confront their own communities, yet they shape the comparison around the concerns of their respective messages. Ezekiel emphasizes pride, abundance, and neglect of the needy, while Isaiah places the name within a broader challenge to rebellion, corrupt leadership, and religious hypocrisy. These emphases overlap, especially in their concern for the moral condition of society, but they should not be collapsed into identical statements. Each prophet selects details that serve the argument being made in the passage.

The Gospels preserve another stage in the history of Sodom's meaning. Jesus refers to Sodom and Gomorrah when warning towns that reject the messengers sent to them. In Matthew 10:14–15 and Luke 10:10–12, the comparison concerns the response to the message and the seriousness of rejecting the opportunity being offered. The remembered destruction of Sodom supplies a familiar measure of judgment, while Jesus declares that the consequences for towns refusing the disciples will be more severe on the day of judgment. The emphasis in these passages is not a fresh description of every offense committed by Sodom; rather, the city serves as an established example through which the gravity of rejection is communicated.

Matthew 11:20–24 develops the warning further when Jesus reproaches towns where many of His mighty works had taken place, yet the people had not repented. He compares their response with that of Sodom and other ancient cities, declaring that the judgment would be more tolerable for Sodom on the day of judgment than for a town that had witnessed such works and remained unresponsive. The comparison depends upon the audience's knowledge of Sodom as a place associated with severe judgment. Jesus applies that memory to accountability, showing that greater exposure to the works and message of God brings greater responsibility. The Gospel tradition therefore uses Sodom not only as a reminder of destruction, but also as a warning about the seriousness of refusing a message accompanied by such evidence.

Luke 17:26–30 places the days of Lot alongside the days of Noah in a teaching about the coming of the Son of Man. The people in Lot's time were eating, drinking, buying, selling, planting, and building until the day Lot left Sodom and destruction came. The ordinary activities are not presented as inherently sinful; their significance lies in the suddenness of judgment and the failure of the people to recognize what was approaching. In this setting, Sodom contributes to Jesus' warning about readiness and the unexpected arrival of judgment. This use differs from the missionary warnings in Matthew and Luke 10, even though all of these passages draw upon the same remembered event.

Second Peter 2:6–9 recalls the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah as an example of what happens to the ungodly, while also emphasizing the rescue of Lot. The passage describes the cities as condemned and reduced to ashes, making them an example for those who would live ungodly lives. It also portrays Lot as distressed by the conduct of lawless people around him and concludes that the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from trial while keeping the unrighteous under punishment for the day of judgment. The writer's purpose includes both warning and assurance: the account demonstrates divine judgment against ungodliness, while Lot's rescue illustrates that the faithful are not beyond God's notice even when surrounded by corruption.

Jude 7 gives Sodom and the surrounding cities a distinct emphasis by connecting their conduct with sexual immorality and the pursuit of different flesh. The verse appears within a sequence of examples involving rebellion and judgment, including the angels who did not keep their proper place. That literary setting matters when interpreting Jude's language, because the reference to different flesh is presented alongside the preceding account of the angels. The passage explicitly retains a sexual dimension in its description of Sodom, but the precise relationship between the city's conduct and the angelic example requires careful attention to the wording and context. The Greek expression should not be treated as though a single phrase resolves every interpretive question. Jude's reference belongs to his own argument and should be read alongside, rather than substituted for, the other biblical uses of Sodom.

When these passages are considered together, the development becomes clearer. Genesis supplies the foundational account of the cities, their wickedness, the violence directed toward the visitors, and their destruction. Ezekiel recalls Sodom in connection with pride, prosperity, and neglect of the vulnerable. Isaiah invokes the city's name to confront rebellion and the separation of religious practice from justice. Jesus uses Sodom as a warning about judgment, accountability, rejection, and readiness. Peter emphasizes ungodliness, lawlessness, judgment, and the rescue of Lot, while Jude explicitly connects the cities with sexual immorality and the pursuit of different flesh. These writers share a remembered story, but each places particular aspects of that story within a distinct message.

The resulting tradition is layered rather than uniform. Sodom can be remembered in connection with violence and sexual wrongdoing, but the prophetic texts also make the city a warning about pride, social neglect, and injustice. The Gospel accounts draw upon its destruction to teach about judgment and responsibility, while the later New Testament letters use the example in discussions of ungodliness, lawlessness, and rebellion. These themes should not be forced into competition, as though recognizing one requires denying the others. Their presence across different passages is precisely what makes the history of Sodom's interpretation worth examining.

Over time, the name Sodom came to carry the accumulated force of these biblical memories. Later readers inherited not only the Genesis narrative, but also the prophetic comparisons and New Testament warnings that had attached further significance to the city. That history helps explain why Sodom became such a powerful reference in religious and moral discussion. It also places a responsibility on modern readers: the meaning of the name cannot be established by isolating one passage or allowing a later use to erase the earlier record. Each source must be heard in its own setting before the wider development can be understood.

WHAT THE DEVELOPMENT OF SODOM'S MEANING SHOWS

The biblical tradition remembers Sodom through several related but distinguishable concerns, including violence, sexual wrongdoing, pride, prosperity without compassion, injustice, rebellion, rejection, lawlessness, and judgment. Later writers drew upon the city's remembered destruction to address the needs of their own audiences, and their different emphases expanded the significance of the story without removing its earlier foundations.

Understanding this development prepares us to consider the historical record as a whole. The next part will bring the evidence together, distinguish conclusions supported by the sources from claims that go beyond them, and assess what can responsibly be said about Sodom and its place in the biblical tradition.
