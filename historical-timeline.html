PART EIGHT

HOW THE MEANING OF SODOM DEVELOPED

By the time the biblical writers began invoking Sodom beyond the Genesis narrative, the city had become a shared point of reference. Its destruction was remembered, but the significance of that memory depended on the questions a later writer needed to address. This part follows that movement from the prophetic books into the New Testament. Rather than repeating the interpretation of each passage already examined in earlier parts, the focus here is on what changes when the same remembered city is brought into a different literary and historical setting.

The development is not a simple progression in which one meaning replaces another. Later writers select particular features of the tradition, and those selections reveal the concerns of their own arguments. The result is an expanding memory: the Genesis account remains the point of reference, while subsequent texts show how its significance could be applied to new audiences and circumstances.

CHAPTER 23

From City to Symbol

“Behold, this was the iniquity of your sister Sodom: pride, overabundance of food, prosperous ease, and idleness were hers and her daughters’; neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. And they were haughty and committed abominable offenses before Me; therefore I removed them when I saw it and I saw fit.”

Ezekiel 16:49–50

WHAT THIS PASSAGE REVEALS

Ezekiel 16 offers a particularly clear example of the way an inherited story could be applied to a later community. The prophet addresses Jerusalem by comparing its conduct with that of Sodom, Samaria, and other figures within the chapter's extended indictment. Sodom's remembered failure becomes a mirror held before Jerusalem. The comparison is deliberately uncomfortable: a city that regarded itself as possessing covenant privilege is confronted with the possibility that its conduct has become worse than the example it would have condemned. Sodom's role in the passage is therefore not simply to recall an ancient punishment. Its name exposes the moral contradiction between a community's claims about itself and the way it treats others.1

The details Ezekiel chooses are significant because they place social responsibility at the centre of his comparison. Abundance is not condemned merely for existing; the prophet condemns a form of prosperity that has become self-satisfied and indifferent. The failure to strengthen the poor and needy reveals what that comfort has produced. Ezekiel also retains language of arrogance and abomination, so his description should not be reduced to an argument about economics alone. His purpose is to expose a comprehensive moral failure in which privilege, pride, and conduct stand together. The passage shows how Sodom's reputation could be used to challenge a later society without claiming that the prophet was retelling every detail of Genesis.

Isaiah 1 uses the names Sodom and Gomorrah in a different prophetic confrontation. The prophet addresses Judah's leadership and worship, describing a community whose religious activity has become unacceptable because wrongdoing remains embedded in public life. The names are applied to the audience as an accusation, not as a geographical identification. Isaiah's comparison makes the familiar reputation of Sodom serve his argument about the distance between religious observance and justice. The command to seek justice and defend those who are vulnerable gives the warning its practical direction. In this setting, the ancient city's name becomes a way of exposing the failure of a present community to live according to the standards it professes.3

These prophetic uses mark an important change in the function of the Sodom tradition. The city is no longer mentioned only as the subject of its own destruction; its remembered fate becomes a language through which prophets can confront other people. Ezekiel directs the comparison toward pride and the neglect of the needy, while Isaiah uses it to challenge rebellion and corrupt worship. Their messages are related, but not interchangeable. The difference matters because it shows that the biblical memory of Sodom was capable of serving more than one prophetic purpose without losing its connection to the earlier narrative.2

In the Gospels, the comparison shifts from the moral condition of a community to the responsibility of people who encounter a message and decide how to respond. During the sending of the disciples, Jesus invokes Sodom when warning towns that refuse to receive the messengers. In Matthew 10:14–15 and Luke 10:10–12, the city provides a measure of the seriousness of rejection. Matthew 11:20–24 applies the comparison to towns that witnessed Jesus' works but did not repent. Luke 17:26–30 uses the days of Lot to describe the ordinary routines continuing until judgment arrived. These passages do not all make the same point: together they show that Jesus could draw on Sodom's established reputation to address rejection, accountability, repentance, and readiness in distinct teaching settings.

The significance of this Gospel use lies partly in what the audience is expected to recognize without a retelling. Jesus does not pause to explain the full narrative of Genesis 19. The name of Sodom already carries enough weight to make the warning intelligible. The comparison assumes a shared memory of judgment and then directs that memory toward the hearers' present response. Sodom has become a reference point through which the seriousness of a decision can be communicated quickly and forcefully.4

Second Peter and Jude demonstrate that this inherited memory continued to function in early Christian writings, although their arguments have different emphases. Second Peter 2 places the destruction of Sodom within a sequence of examples concerning judgment and deliverance. The account serves the writer's warning about ungodliness, while Lot's rescue contributes to the assurance that God knows how to deliver the righteous. Jude 7, by contrast, explicitly associates Sodom and the surrounding cities with sexual immorality and the pursuit of different flesh. Jude places this reference beside the preceding example of angels who abandoned their proper position. The relationship between those examples is important to the interpretation of the passage, and the Greek phrase *sarkos heteras* should be considered in that context rather than treated as a self-contained answer to every question about the verse.5

The difference between Peter and Jude is not a contradiction that must be resolved by making one writer's emphasis control the other. Each author uses the remembered cities within a distinct sequence of examples and a distinct warning. Peter foregrounds judgment and deliverance; Jude foregrounds sexual transgression and the surrounding pattern of rebellion. Their references show that the tradition remained active and adaptable, while also reminding readers that a shared reference does not guarantee an identical interpretation.

By this stage, Sodom had become more than a location recalled in an ancient account. Its name could summon a known story and allow a later writer to apply that story to a particular question. The prophetic books use the city to confront the moral failures of later communities; the Gospels use it to warn hearers about the consequences of rejecting a message or failing to respond; and the New Testament letters incorporate it into arguments about judgment, deliverance, and transgression. The significance of Sodom grew through these uses, not because the earlier narrative disappeared, but because later writers continued to find meaning in its remembered fate.6

That history also explains why later discussions of Sodom can become confused when they treat every biblical reference as though it were making one comprehensive statement. A prophetic accusation against social arrogance, a Gospel warning about rejection, and an epistolary example of judgment are related through their shared reference to Sodom, but they address different questions. The careful reader must preserve those distinctions before drawing conclusions about the tradition as a whole.

THE DEVELOPMENT AND THE HISTORICAL RECORD

The movement from Genesis through the prophets and into the New Testament shows how Sodom's remembered destruction became a recurring point of reference. Later writers did not simply repeat the original account; they applied its memory to the responsibilities and failures of their own audiences. The development is best understood as an accumulation of distinct interpretations grounded in a shared story.
