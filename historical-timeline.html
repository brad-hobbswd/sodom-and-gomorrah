PART EIGHT

HOW THE MEANING OF SODOM DEVELOPED

The story of Sodom did not remain frozen in the form in which it first appeared in the biblical tradition. As generations passed, later writers remembered the city and used its story to address the circumstances of their own communities.

This does not mean that every later writer invented a new Sodom. It means that an already familiar story could carry several layers of meaning at once.

CHAPTER 16

From City to Symbol

Something important happened as the centuries passed.

Sodom stopped being remembered only as the name of two ancient cities.

It became a symbol.

For Ezekiel, Sodom represented pride and social neglect. His description in Ezekiel 16:49–50 emphasizes arrogance, abundance, comfortable prosperity, and the failure to help the poor and needy.

For Isaiah, Sodom represented rebellion and injustice. Isaiah could invoke the name when confronting a society whose religious practices had become disconnected from justice and righteous conduct.

For Jesus, Sodom represented judgment. When Jesus compared towns that rejected His messengers with Sodom and Gomorrah, He relied on a story His audience already knew.

For Peter, Sodom represented ungodliness and lawlessness. The destruction of the cities became an example of divine judgment against people who rejected God's ways.

For Jude, Sodom represented sexual immorality connected with “different flesh.” Jude's language is especially important because he places Sodom alongside the account of rebellious angels and connects the city's behavior with sexual wrongdoing.

These writers were not necessarily contradicting each other.

They were using an old story to speak to new generations.

That is one of the most important historical facts about Sodom.

The meaning of Sodom developed as the biblical tradition developed.

The development did not erase the earlier meanings. Instead, each later reference added another layer to the memory.

Genesis preserved the foundational story of the city and its destruction.

Ezekiel emphasized the social conditions surrounding Sodom's corruption.

Isaiah transformed the name into a warning against injustice and religious hypocrisy.

Jesus used Sodom as a familiar example of judgment.

Peter used the destruction as evidence within his teaching about ungodliness and divine judgment.

Jude connected the story with sexual immorality and the unusual identity of the beings involved in the Genesis narrative.

Seen together, these references show why it is difficult to reduce Sodom to one sentence.

The biblical tradition remembers Sodom through several lenses. The city can be associated with sexual violence, social injustice, pride, prosperity without compassion, rebellion, lawlessness, rejection, and divine judgment.

These themes should not automatically be treated as competing definitions. They are part of a developing tradition in which later writers repeatedly returned to the same remembered event.

The name Sodom eventually carried the weight of that accumulated memory.

WHAT THE DEVELOPMENT OF SODOM'S MEANING SHOWS

Sodom became a symbol because later generations continued to remember the city and apply its story to their own circumstances. Different biblical writers emphasized different aspects of the tradition, but the earlier story remained the foundation for those later interpretations.

The historical record therefore gives us a layered picture rather than a single isolated definition. Understanding that development is essential if we want to understand what Sodom meant across the biblical tradition.
