PART TWO • THE ANCIENT WORLD

The World Behind the Story

Before we examine what happened to Sodom and Gomorrah, we need to understand the world in which the story is set. Genesis places Sodom in the world of Abraham and Lot, a world of city-states, tribal families, agriculture, trade, warfare, migration, hospitality, and strong obligations between households.

These customs were not identical to those of the modern world. Words such as family, household, stranger, covenant, authority, honor, and hospitality carried social responsibilities that were much broader than their modern equivalents.

Abraham, Lot, and the Land

Genesis introduces Abraham as a man who leaves his homeland and travels into the land of Canaan. His nephew Lot travels with him. Their households grow, and the land cannot comfortably support all their possessions and livestock together.

Genesis 13 describes the resulting separation. Lot looks toward the well watered plain of the Jordan and chooses that region for himself. The text then identifies the cities of the plain and gives an early warning about Sodom's reputation.

Genesis 13:13

“Now the men of Sodom were wicked, sinners against the LORD, exceedingly so.”

This statement is important because it occurs before the events of Genesis 19. The narrative does not introduce Sodom as an ordinary city that suddenly becomes corrupt on one particular night. Its reputation for wickedness is already part of the story.

The Cities of the Plain

Sodom should be understood within a landscape containing several settlements. Genesis names Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, Zeboiim, and Bela, which is also called Zoar. The narrative connects these cities with the Jordan Valley and the surrounding plain.

The precise location of Sodom remains debated. Several proposed locations have been discussed by historians, archaeologists, and biblical scholars, but there is no universally accepted archaeological identification of the city.

Historical caution: The biblical narrative gives geographical clues, but those clues do not allow us to state with certainty where Sodom stood. A responsible historical study should distinguish between what the biblical text says, what archaeology can demonstrate, and what later traditions have proposed.

A World of Hospitality

Hospitality was a serious social responsibility in the ancient Near East. Travelers could be vulnerable when they entered unfamiliar territory, especially when they lacked the protection of a local household.

Hospitality involved more than being polite. Providing food, water, shelter, protection, and safe passage could become matters of life and death. A household that received a vulnerable stranger could assume significant responsibility for that person's safety.

This background becomes especially important in Genesis 18 and 19. Abraham welcomes visitors, while Lot receives the visitors who enter Sodom. The contrast between welcoming strangers and the behavior of the men who surround Lot's house becomes a major part of the narrative.

Honor, Shame, and the Household

Ancient societies often organized social life around households and extended families. A household's reputation could affect its security, relationships, marriage arrangements, economic position, and standing within the community.

Honor and shame were therefore not merely private emotions. They could function as powerful social forces. Public humiliation, violence, sexual violation, and the abuse of a guest could carry consequences far beyond the immediate victim.

This does not excuse the behavior described in Genesis. Instead, understanding the social world helps us see why the narrative presents the events at Lot's house as such a serious violation.

Warfare and Political Power

Genesis 14 places Sodom within a larger political world. Kings and city-states form alliances, rebel against foreign rulers, and fight over territory and control.

This chapter reminds us that Sodom was not presented as an isolated settlement. It belonged to a network of cities and political powers whose relationships could change through war, rebellion, trade, and shifting alliances.

The ancient world of Genesis was therefore both local and international. A decision made by one city could affect neighboring cities, and military conflict could move through an entire region.

Sexuality in the Ancient World

One of the most important historical cautions is that ancient people did not organize sexual identity using exactly the same categories commonly used today. The modern concept of sexual orientation developed much later.

Ancient texts more often discuss sexual behavior through categories such as marriage, kinship, status, gender, honor, purity, domination, fertility, and religious practice.

This distinction matters when reading Genesis, Leviticus, and the later biblical references to Sodom. We should not automatically place modern identity categories into ancient passages without first asking what the original writers were actually discussing.

Historical principle: Describing an ancient text accurately does not require deciding every modern debate about sexuality. The first task is to determine what the ancient text says, what its language means in context, and how its earliest audiences may have understood it.

Family, Covenant, and Authority

Family relationships were central to ancient society. Genesis repeatedly describes decisions involving parents, children, spouses, brothers, sisters, servants, relatives, and household leaders.

Covenant also carries an important meaning throughout the biblical world. Covenants established obligations between people and, in biblical theology, between God and His people. They could involve promises, loyalty, protection, responsibility, and consequences for breaking the agreement.

Authority operated within households, communities, kingdoms, and religious structures. Later biblical writers use Sodom as an example when discussing rebellion, injustice, sexual behavior, social responsibility, and rejection of divine authority.

Why the Historical Setting Matters

The story of Sodom cannot be separated from the world that produced it. Genesis is telling an ancient story to an ancient audience, using social categories that may not map neatly onto modern categories.

That does not make the story irrelevant today. It makes historical context more important.

If we want to understand Sodom carefully, we must allow each biblical writer to speak within his own historical setting before we bring the texts together.
