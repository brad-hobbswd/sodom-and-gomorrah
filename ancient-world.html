PART ONE

THE ANCIENT WORLD

Before we enter the story of Sodom and Gomorrah itself, we need to slow down and look at the world in which the story takes place. Genesis does not describe a modern society. It describes a world organized around households, extended families, tribal relationships, local rulers, agriculture, livestock, trade, warfare, and strong expectations about how people should treat one another.

Understanding that world does not answer every question about Sodom. It does, however, give us the historical setting we need before we begin examining the biblical passages themselves.

CHAPTER 1

Before Sodom: The World of Abraham

“Let there be no strife, I pray thee, between me and thee, and between my herdmen and thy herdmen; for we be brethren. Is not the whole land before thee? separate thyself, I pray thee, from me: if thou wilt take the left hand, then I will go to the right; or if thou depart to the right hand, then I will go to the left.”

Genesis 13:8–9

WHAT THIS PASSAGE ESTABLISHES

Before we can understand Sodom, we need to understand the world in which the story takes place. This passage introduces the relationship between Abraham and Lot and shows how land, livestock, family ties, and the need to prevent conflict shaped their decisions.

The world described in Genesis was very different from the modern world. People did not live within the systems of government, law enforcement, hospitals, social welfare programs, and international organizations that many people depend upon today.

Communities depended heavily upon family relationships, tribal connections, alliances, and local rulers. A person's family could provide protection, economic support, a place to live, and a connection to the larger community. Losing those connections could leave a person extremely vulnerable.

This is especially important when we think about travel. Roads could be dangerous, journeys were often long, and travelers could find themselves far away from the people who knew them. A person arriving in a strange city might have no relatives nearby and no institution responsible for protecting them.

A stranger therefore occupied a very different position from someone who belonged to the local community. The stranger depended upon the willingness of others to provide food, water, shelter, and protection.

This is why hospitality was so important throughout the ancient Near East. Welcoming a traveler was not simply a matter of being polite. Hospitality could involve serious social responsibilities.

A host could be expected to provide food and shelter and to protect a guest who had entered the household. The treatment of a stranger could also affect the honor of the household receiving that person.

Family and household were also central to daily life. Abraham's household included relatives, servants, livestock, and other people connected to him. Lot was Abraham's nephew, and their relationship is part of the larger story that eventually brings Lot toward Sodom.

Genesis repeatedly shows people making decisions based upon land, family, security, resources, and relationships. These decisions can look very different when read from a modern perspective because the social world behind them was different.

Honor and shame were also powerful social forces. A person's behavior could affect not only the individual but the reputation of an entire household. Public humiliation, violence, the mistreatment of guests, and violations of household boundaries could therefore have consequences far beyond the immediate event.

None of this excuses the actions described later in Genesis. Instead, it helps us understand why the narrative treats the treatment of strangers as a serious matter.

This background becomes extremely important when we eventually arrive at Genesis 19. The events in that chapter do not happen in a historical vacuum. They happen in a society where hospitality, household responsibility, honor, protection, and the treatment of outsiders carried enormous significance.

CHAPTER 2

Sodom Before the Destruction

“Lot lifted up his eyes, and saw all the plain of the Jordan, that it was well watered everywhere, before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah, like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt, as you go to Zoar.”

Genesis 13:10

WHAT THIS VERSE REVEALS

Genesis introduces Sodom before its destruction. The city is not first presented as a pile of ruins or simply as a symbol of judgment. It is presented as a real place within a fertile region where people lived, worked, raised animals, formed political relationships, and competed for resources. The verse describes the region as well watered, comparing it to the garden of the LORD and the land of Egypt. That description presents the area as attractive and productive.

This detail is important.

Sodom was not initially presented as a barren wasteland. The surrounding region was associated with fertility and prosperity, which helps explain why Lot looked toward the Jordan Valley and chose that area.

Lot's decision was practical. He saw land that appeared capable of supporting his household and possessions. Genesis presents the choice before it reveals the full moral condition of the city.

“Now the men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinners against the LORD.”

Genesis 13:13

This is the first major warning about the city's moral condition. The narrative places the warning beside the description of the region's prosperity, reminding the reader that attractive surroundings do not necessarily indicate a community's moral health.

The text does not yet give us a complete explanation of their wickedness. It does not stop and provide a detailed list of every sin committed by the people of Sodom.

That is historically significant. The reader is first told that Sodom has a serious moral problem, and the narrative later provides scenes that help us understand the nature of that corruption.

Genesis 14 also places Sodom within a larger political world. The city is connected with other kings and cities in the region, showing that Sodom was not isolated from the surrounding world.

This matters because the biblical story is not only about one city. It is also about a network of cities, rulers, families, alliances, conflicts, and communities living within the same region.

When we eventually reach the destruction of Sodom, therefore, we are not watching the destruction of an abstract symbol. We are reading the story of a community that the biblical writers believed had become deeply corrupt.

At this stage, however, we should be careful. Genesis has told us that Sodom is wicked and that its sin is serious, but it has not yet given us every reason. We should allow the narrative to develop before deciding that one particular sin explains everything.

CHAPTER 3

The Outcry Against Sodom

“And the LORD said, Because the cry of Sodom and Gomorrah is great, and because their sin is very grievous; I will go down now, and see whether they have done altogether according to the cry of it, which is come unto me; and if not, I will know.”

Genesis 18:20–21

WHAT THIS PASSAGE REVEALS

Genesis 18 takes the story further. God tells Abraham that the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great and that their sin is very serious. The biblical text places this declaration before the events in the city, establishing the seriousness of the accusation before the narrative shows the reader what happens next.

The language of an “outcry” is important in the ancient biblical world. An outcry is more than a general statement that people are unhappy. In biblical literature, cries for help can be associated with people suffering under oppression, violence, injustice, exploitation, or other forms of wrongdoing.

The language therefore raises an important question: Who is crying out, and what has happened to them?

Genesis does not give us a complete answer in one sentence. Instead, the story unfolds. The reader is invited to watch what happens when the visitors enter Sodom and to compare the behavior of the people inside the city with the behavior expected of those who receive strangers.

The text presents Sodom as a place where something terrible had been happening for long enough that an outcry had reached God. The issue is therefore larger than a single event on a single night.

The story is moving toward judgment, but it also introduces another important theme: divine justice.

Abraham does not simply accept the coming judgment without question. He asks whether the Judge of all the earth will do what is right. His conversation with God shows that the story is concerned not only with judgment but with whether judgment itself is just.

Abraham's concern also helps establish an important biblical principle. The destruction of a wicked society is not presented as something that happens because God simply loses patience. The narrative portrays God as responding to a grave situation and examining what has happened.

But the reader still has not been given a complete list of Sodom's sins. That comes through the story itself and through later biblical writers. Genesis will give us the events surrounding Lot and his visitors. Later prophets will remember Sodom in different ways. Other biblical writers will emphasize additional aspects of the city's wickedness.

This is why the historical journey must continue beyond Genesis 18 and 19. We need to examine the story itself first, and then follow the memory of Sodom through the rest of the biblical record.

THE NEXT STEP

We have now established the historical setting. We have seen the world of Abraham, the prosperity associated with the region of Sodom, the city's reputation for wickedness, and the great outcry that precedes judgment.

Now we can turn to Genesis itself and examine what happened when the visitors came to Sodom.
