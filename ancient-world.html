PART ONE

THE ANCIENT WORLD

The account of Sodom emerges from a particular time and place, shaped by the political, economic, and social realities of the ancient Near East. This first part establishes that setting through the world of Abraham and Lot, the landscape surrounding Sodom, and the events that precede the city's destruction. The historical comparisons in this part provide cultural context; they should not be mistaken for proof that every custom described can be assigned to a precisely dated patriarchal period.1

The chapters begin with the people and circumstances surrounding the narrative, then move toward the biblical account of the city's growing reputation and the outcry that brings its conduct before God.

CHAPTER 1

Before Sodom: The World of Abraham

“Let there be no strife, I pray thee, between me and thee, and between my herdmen and thy herdmen; for we be brethren. Is not the whole land before thee? separate thyself, I pray thee, from me: if thou wilt take the left hand, then I will go to the right; or if thou depart to the right hand, then I will go to the left.”

Genesis 13:8–9

WHAT THIS PASSAGE ESTABLISHES

Genesis 13:8–9 places Abraham and Lot at a point of practical and relational tension. Their households have grown, and the land cannot adequately sustain both groups together. Abraham's proposal to separate is therefore more than a personal preference: it is an attempt to resolve a dispute between their herdsmen while preserving the bond between the two men. His words, “for we be brethren,” make that family relationship central to the decision.

The world described in Genesis was very different from the modern world. Ancient communities did have rulers, laws, courts, and systems of political authority, but they did not have modern institutions such as professional police forces, hospitals, public welfare agencies, or international organizations. The ways people obtained protection, resolved disputes, and secured assistance were shaped by the local authorities and social relationships available to them.2

Communities depended heavily upon family relationships, tribal connections, alliances, and local rulers. A person's family could provide protection, economic support, a place to live, and a connection to the larger community. Losing those connections could leave a person extremely vulnerable.3

This is especially important when we think about travel. Roads could be dangerous, journeys were often long, and travelers could find themselves far away from the people who knew them. A person arriving in a strange city might have no relatives nearby and no established personal connection to those able to offer protection.4

A stranger therefore occupied a very different position from someone who belonged to the local community. The stranger depended upon the willingness of others to provide food, water, shelter, and protection.

Hospitality was therefore an important social responsibility throughout the ancient Near East. Welcoming a traveler involved more than ordinary politeness, since the host could be expected to provide food, shelter, and protection to someone who had entered the household.5

A host could be expected to provide food and shelter and to protect a guest who had entered the household. The treatment of a stranger could also affect the honor of the household receiving that person.6

Family and household were also central to daily life. Abraham's household included relatives, servants, livestock, and other people connected to him. Lot was Abraham's nephew, and their relationship is part of the larger story that eventually brings Lot toward Sodom.

Genesis repeatedly shows people making decisions based upon land, family, security, resources, and relationships. These decisions can look very different when read from a modern perspective because the social world behind them was different.

Honor and shame were also powerful social forces. A person's behavior could affect not only the individual but the reputation of an entire household. Public humiliation, violence, the mistreatment of guests, and violations of household boundaries could therefore have consequences far beyond the immediate event. These concepts are useful interpretive lenses, although they should not be treated as a single, uniform code shared by every ancient society.7

None of this excuses the actions described later in Genesis. Instead, it helps us understand why the narrative treats the treatment of strangers as a serious matter.

This background becomes essential when we arrive at Genesis 19, because the events in that chapter do not happen in a historical vacuum. They unfold within a society where hospitality, household responsibility, honor, protection, and the treatment of outsiders carried considerable social significance.

CHAPTER 2

Sodom Before the Destruction

“Lot lifted up his eyes, and saw all the plain of the Jordan, that it was well watered everywhere, before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah, like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt, as you go to Zoar.”

Genesis 13:10

WHAT THIS VERSE REVEALS

Genesis introduces Sodom before its destruction. The city is not first presented as a pile of ruins or simply as a symbol of judgment. It is presented as a real place within a fertile region where people lived, worked, raised animals, formed political relationships, and competed for resources. The verse describes the region as well watered, comparing it to the garden of the LORD and the land of Egypt. That description presents the area as attractive and productive within the narrator's comparison.8

This description matters because Sodom is not initially presented as a barren wasteland. The surrounding region is associated with fertility and prosperity, helping explain why Lot looked toward the Jordan Valley and chose that area.

Lot's decision was practical: he saw land that appeared capable of supporting his household and possessions, and Genesis presents his choice before revealing the full moral condition of the city.

“Now the men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinners against the LORD.”

Genesis 13:13

This is the first major warning about the city's moral condition. The narrative places the warning beside the description of the region's prosperity, reminding the reader that attractive surroundings do not necessarily indicate a community's moral health.

At this point, the text does not provide a complete explanation of Sodom's wickedness or a detailed list of every sin committed by its people. Instead, the reader is first told that the city has a serious moral problem, while the narrative gradually supplies scenes that help clarify the nature of that corruption.

Genesis 14 also places Sodom within a larger political world. The city is connected with other kings and cities in the region, showing that Sodom was not isolated from the surrounding world.

The biblical story is therefore not concerned with one city in isolation, but with a wider network of cities, rulers, families, alliances, conflicts, and communities living within the same region.9

When we eventually reach the destruction of Sodom, therefore, we are not watching the destruction of an abstract symbol. We are reading the story of a community that the biblical writers believed had become deeply corrupt.

At this stage, however, we should be careful. Genesis has told us that Sodom is wicked and that its sin is serious, but it has not yet given us every reason. We should allow the narrative to develop before deciding that one particular sin explains everything.

CHAPTER 3

The Outcry Against Sodom

“And the LORD said, Because the cry of Sodom and Gomorrah is great, and because their sin is very grievous; I will go down now, and see whether they have done altogether according to the cry of it, which is come unto me; and if not, I will know.”

Genesis 18:20–21

WHAT THIS PASSAGE REVEALS

Genesis 18 takes the story further. God tells Abraham that the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great and that their sin is very serious. The biblical text places this declaration before the events in the city, establishing the seriousness of the accusation before the narrative shows the reader what happens next.

The language of an “outcry” is important in the ancient biblical world. An outcry is more than a general statement that people are unhappy. In biblical literature, cries for help can be associated with people suffering under oppression, violence, injustice, exploitation, or other forms of wrongdoing. Similar language appears when the Israelites cry out under oppression in Egypt and when biblical law addresses the cries of vulnerable people who have been mistreated.10

The language therefore raises an important question that the narrative invites readers to consider: who is crying out, and what has happened to them?

Genesis does not identify a particular group of victims behind the outcry in these verses, nor does it give us a complete answer in one sentence. Instead, the story unfolds. The reader is invited to watch what happens when the visitors enter Sodom and to compare the behavior of the people inside the city with the behavior expected of those who receive strangers.

The text presents Sodom as a place where something terrible had been happening for long enough that an outcry had reached God. The issue is therefore larger than a single event on a single night.

As the story moves toward judgment, it also introduces the essential theme of divine justice. Abraham does not simply accept the coming judgment without question; he asks whether the Judge of all the earth will do what is right, showing that the narrative is concerned not only with judgment but also with whether judgment itself is just.

Abraham's concern also helps establish an important biblical principle. The destruction of a wicked society is not presented as something that happens because God simply loses patience. The narrative portrays God as responding to a grave situation and examining what has happened.

Abraham's exchange with God does not resolve every question about the accusations against Sodom. Instead, it places the coming events within a larger concern for justice: judgment must correspond to what has actually happened. The narrative now moves from the announcement of the outcry to the encounter in the city, where the reader will see the conduct that gives the accusation its immediate narrative setting.

Notes and References

  1. The precise historical setting and chronology of the patriarchal narratives remain subjects of scholarly discussion. Comparative studies of the ancient Near East can illuminate cultural patterns, but they do not independently establish the date or historicity of each event in Genesis. See John H. Walton, Ancient Near Eastern Thought and the Old Testament, 2nd ed. (Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2018), especially the discussion of comparative method and the conceptual world of the Hebrew Bible. ↩
  2. Ancient Near Eastern societies had political authorities, rulers, legal traditions, and mechanisms for resolving disputes. The distinction here is between those ancient arrangements and modern state institutions, not between government and an absence of government. See Walton, Ancient Near Eastern Thought, and Victor H. Matthews, Manners & Customs in the Bible: An Illustrated Guide to Daily Life in Bible Times, 3rd ed. (Peabody, MA: Hendrickson Publishers, 2006). ↩
  3. Kinship, household membership, land, labor, and patronage were important dimensions of social and economic life in the biblical world. These structures varied across time and place. See Philip J. King and Lawrence E. Stager, Life in Biblical Israel (Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 2001), and Matthews, Manners & Customs in the Bible. ↩
  4. The vulnerability of travelers and the importance of local relationships are recurring themes in biblical narratives. Genesis 18:1–8 and 19:1–3 provide the immediate narrative examples of strangers being received into a household. Matthews, Manners & Customs in the Bible, provides broader cultural background. ↩
  5. For the role of hospitality and household customs in biblical interpretation, see Genesis 18:1–8; 19:1–3; Judges 19:15–21; and Matthews, Manners & Customs in the Bible. These texts show hospitality as a narrative and social concern; they should not be taken to mean that every community followed one identical rule in every circumstance. ↩
  6. Household honor, kinship, and social standing are important themes in studies of biblical social life. See King and Stager, Life in Biblical Israel; and Matthews, Manners & Customs in the Bible. The application of honor and shame language to a particular passage should be grounded in the passage's details rather than assumed in advance. ↩
  7. “Honor and shame” are interpretive terms used in social-scientific readings of ancient texts. They can clarify public reputation and household relationships, but they are not a complete explanation of every action or a universal code shared without variation across the ancient Near East. See Walton, Ancient Near Eastern Thought, and Matthews, Manners & Customs in the Bible. ↩
  8. Genesis 13:10 describes the Jordan plain through the narrator's comparison with the garden of the LORD and the land of Egypt. This is the biblical text's portrayal of the landscape; the verse alone does not establish the precise location of Sodom or provide an archaeological identification. See Genesis 13:10. ↩
  9. Genesis 14:1–12 situates Sodom among a coalition of kings and describes a regional conflict. The passage supplies the literary and political setting for the story; identifying the named rulers with specific archaeological figures requires separate evidence and remains a distinct historical question. ↩
  10. Compare Exodus 2:23–25; 3:7–9; 22:21–27; Deuteronomy 24:14–15; and Job 34:28. These passages connect cries or appeals with suffering, oppression, and the treatment of vulnerable people. This comparison establishes a biblical pattern of usage; Genesis 18:20–21 itself does not specify the identity of those whose outcry has reached God. ↩
