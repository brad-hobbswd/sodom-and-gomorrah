PART NINE

WHAT THE HISTORICAL RECORD ALLOWS US TO SAY

After examining the biblical texts from several periods, we can now step back and ask a simple question: What can the evidence actually support?

This question matters because Sodom has often been reduced to a single explanation. One interpretation may emphasize sexual behavior. Another may emphasize poverty, injustice, pride, or hospitality. Still another may focus almost entirely on divine judgment.

The ancient sources are more complicated than any one of these explanations by itself.

They preserve different memories of the same city and its destruction. Those memories overlap, but they also emphasize different features. A responsible historical reading should therefore distinguish what the texts clearly say from what later interpreters have added.

CHAPTER 17

The Conclusions the Evidence Supports

The evidence allows us to say several things with confidence, while also showing us where caution is necessary.

Sodom was portrayed as a wicked society before its destruction.

Genesis does not introduce Sodom as an innocent city that suddenly became guilty because of one event. Genesis 13:13 describes the people of Sodom as wicked and as sinners against the LORD. Genesis 18:20 describes the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah as great and their sin as very serious.

The destruction story therefore belongs to a larger biblical portrayal of a society already understood as deeply corrupt.

Genesis 19 contains an attempted sexual assault against male visitors.

This part of the story should not be removed or ignored. The men of Sodom surround Lot's house and demand that the male visitors be brought outside, and Lot's response makes clear that he understands the demand as a sexual threat.

The passage also presents the threatened act as violent and coercive. It is not a description of a modern relationship between consenting adults. The scene involves intimidation, a mob, abuse of strangers, and an attempted sexual assault.

That distinction is historically important. The text clearly contains male-on-male sexual violence, but it does not use the modern categories of sexual orientation or sexual identity.

The story also contains violence, hostility toward strangers, and a serious violation of hospitality.

Hospitality was an important social value in the ancient world. A traveler who entered an unfamiliar community could depend heavily on the protection of a household and its host.

Genesis 19 turns that expectation upside down. Instead of protecting the visitors, the men of the city attempt to seize them. Violence replaces hospitality, and the strangers become targets.

This means the story cannot honestly be reduced to one isolated sexual act. Sexual violence, mob violence, hostility toward outsiders, and the rejection of expected hospitality are all present in the narrative.

Ezekiel identifies pride, prosperity, and failure to help the poor and needy as major characteristics of Sodom.

Ezekiel 16:49–50 gives one of the clearest descriptions of Sodom outside Genesis. The prophet names pride, abundant food, prosperous ease, and failure to strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. He then describes the people as haughty and as committing abominations.

This passage is especially important because it shows that the memory of Sodom was not limited to the events of Genesis 19. Later biblical reflection understood the city's corruption in social and moral terms as well.

Leviticus contains laws concerning male same-sex intercourse, although those laws belong to Israel's covenant legislation rather than the Genesis story itself.

Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 contain explicit prohibitions concerning sexual intercourse between males within Israel's holiness and covenant legislation.

Those passages should be taken seriously for what they actually are. They are laws given within Israel's covenant community. They do not retell Genesis 19, and they do not state that these laws were the single reason Sodom was destroyed.

The existence of these laws is historically relevant to the development of biblical sexual ethics, but the passages should not be collapsed into the Sodom narrative.

Jude connects Sodom with sexual immorality and “different flesh” while discussing angels.

Jude 6 describes angels who did not keep their proper position, and Jude 7 immediately turns to Sodom and Gomorrah. Jude describes the cities as having given themselves over to sexual immorality and having gone after “different flesh.”

The Greek phrase is sarkos heteras. The word sarkos means flesh, while heteras means different, other, or another kind. In the immediate context, the reference to angels in the preceding verse makes the connection significant.

A strong contextual reading is that Jude understood the men of Sodom as seeking sexual contact with beings of a different kind, corresponding to the heavenly visitors in the Genesis tradition.

However, the Greek phrase itself does not mean “bestiality.” The word heteras does not mean animal or beast. Therefore, describing Jude as teaching “ontological bestiality” goes beyond what the vocabulary itself establishes.

Peter connects Sodom with ungodliness, lawlessness, corrupt desire, and rebellion.

Second Peter 2 uses the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah as an example of divine judgment. Peter speaks of ungodly people, lawless conduct, corrupt desire, and rejection of authority.

Peter therefore preserves several dimensions of the Sodom tradition at the same time. The cities are remembered as examples of moral corruption and as examples of judgment.

Jesus uses Sodom as an example of judgment.

In Matthew 10:14–15, Jesus compares towns that reject His messengers with Sodom and Gomorrah. The comparison emphasizes rejection and judgment.

Jesus does not say that rejection of hospitality was the only sin of Sodom. Instead, He uses the remembered fate of the cities to warn people about the seriousness of rejecting the message and messengers sent to them.

David and Jonathan demonstrate an extraordinarily deep relationship between two men, although Scripture does not explicitly identify their relationship as sexual.

The relationship between David and Jonathan deserves careful treatment because the biblical language is unusually strong. First Samuel 18 describes Jonathan's soul as being bound to David's, and Jonathan is said to love David as himself. Their covenant is accompanied by the exchange of clothing and weapons.

After Jonathan's death, David says that Jonathan's love for him was wonderful and that it surpassed the love of women.

These passages have led some readers and scholars to consider whether the relationship may have included romantic or homoerotic dimensions. Others understand the language within the ancient world of covenant friendship, loyalty, kinship, and political alliance.

What the text does not do is explicitly describe sexual activity between the two men. A responsible historical reading should therefore acknowledge the extraordinary intimacy of the relationship without claiming that Scripture proves a sexual relationship.

CHAPTER 18

What We Should Not Claim

A careful study is defined not only by what it affirms, but also by what it refuses to claim without evidence.

We should not claim that Sodom's story is exclusively about homosexuality. Genesis 19 describes an attempted sexual assault between males, but the wider biblical tradition also speaks about pride, prosperity, neglect of the needy, injustice, violence, lawlessness, rebellion, and judgment.

We should not claim that Sodom had nothing to do with sexual wrongdoing. Genesis 19 contains a sexual threat, and Jude explicitly connects Sodom with sexual immorality.

We should not claim that Ezekiel 16:49–50 gives a complete list of every sin committed by Sodom. Ezekiel highlights particular characteristics. His list does not erase the other biblical traditions.

We should not claim that Jude's phrase “different flesh” automatically means bestiality. The phrase indicates different or other flesh, and the surrounding reference to angels is important, but the Greek vocabulary itself does not identify animals.

We should not claim that Deuteronomy 29:23–27 directly says Sodom was destroyed because of idolatry. The passage uses the destruction of Sodom as a comparison while its immediate discussion of Israel's judgment concerns abandoning the covenant and worshiping other gods.

We should not claim that David and Jonathan are explicitly described as sexual partners. The biblical text gives us an unusually intimate relationship, but it does not directly state that they had sex.

These boundaries do not weaken the study. They make it stronger because they keep the conclusions connected to the evidence rather than to assumptions that the texts themselves do not establish.

CHAPTER 19

A More Complete Historical Picture

Taken together, these sources give us a much more complicated historical picture than either extreme usually allows.

Sodom is not simply a story about one modern category of sexual identity, but neither is it a story from which every reference to sexual behavior can be removed.

It is a story about a wicked society that becomes a lasting symbol of corruption and judgment.

Genesis gives us the narrative of the city, its visitors, its violence, its sexual threat, and its destruction.

Ezekiel remembers Sodom through the language of pride, prosperity, social neglect, and arrogance.

Leviticus provides Israel's own covenant laws concerning sexual behavior between males, while remaining a separate legal tradition from Genesis 19.

Isaiah uses Sodom as a prophetic symbol in a larger argument about rebellion, religious hypocrisy, and injustice.

Jesus uses Sodom as a warning about judgment and the rejection of God's messengers.

Peter remembers Sodom as an example of ungodliness, lawlessness, corrupt desire, and divine judgment.

Jude connects the cities with sexual immorality and “different flesh” while placing the story beside the tradition of rebellious angels.

David and Jonathan provide a separate example showing that the biblical world could describe an exceptionally deep bond between two men without explicitly defining that bond as sexual.

The result is not a simple answer designed to support one modern argument.

The result is a historical record made up of layers.

THE CONCLUSION

Sodom became one of the Bible's most powerful symbols because its story could carry many dimensions at once: wickedness, violence, sexual wrongdoing, pride, prosperity without compassion, neglect of the vulnerable, rejection, rebellion, lawlessness, and judgment.

The evidence does not require us to choose between every one of these themes. It requires us to read each source carefully, understand its historical setting, and allow each text to say what it actually says.

The most responsible conclusion is therefore not that Sodom had only one sin. It is that the biblical tradition remembered Sodom as a deeply corrupt society whose story became a lasting warning about what happens when power, violence, pride, injustice, and rebellion become normal.
