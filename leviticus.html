PART FOUR

SODOM AND ISRAEL'S LAWS

The study now turns from the story of Sodom and Ezekiel's later use of its memory to a different kind of biblical material: the laws in Leviticus. Genesis recounts the events surrounding the city, while Ezekiel uses Sodom as part of a prophetic challenge to Jerusalem, particularly concerning pride, abundance, and the neglect of those in need. Leviticus has another purpose. It presents instructions for Israel as a covenant people, addressing worship, family relationships, sexual conduct, justice, and the responsibilities that shape communal life. Reading these texts together can be valuable, but their different literary forms must remain clear. A narrative describes events; legislation sets out expectations and consequences for a community.

This part focuses on Leviticus 18 and 20, examining their specific prohibitions, their surrounding instructions, and their place within Israel's holiness legislation. Both chapters include a prohibition concerning male same-sex intercourse, and chapter 20 also records a penalty. Those statements deserve to be addressed directly and in their own context. At the same time, neither chapter identifies its laws as the reason Sodom was destroyed. The purpose here is to understand what Leviticus says, how its laws function within the Torah, and how later readers have brought these passages into conversation with the story of Sodom without confusing their different settings.

CHAPTER 12

Leviticus and the Holiness of Israel

“You shall not lie with a man as with a woman; it is an abomination.”

Leviticus 18:22

Leviticus is part of the Torah's account of Israel's life before God. Its instructions cover sacrifices and offerings, the responsibilities of priests, ritual purity, sacred occasions, ethical conduct, and relationships within the community. These subjects may appear different from one another, yet the book brings them together through a recurring concern: Israel is to live as a people set apart for the God who has entered into covenant with them. Holiness is not presented as a matter limited to worship or private devotion. The laws also shape how the community is ordered and how its members are expected to treat one another. This wider purpose provides the setting for the instructions examined in this part.1

Chapter 18 makes this concern especially clear. Its opening instructions tell Israel not to follow the practices associated with Egypt or Canaan, but to live according to the statutes given by the LORD. The chapter then describes prohibited sexual relationships and acts, placing the individual commands within a larger call to covenant faithfulness. Near its conclusion, the text speaks of the land becoming defiled by the practices it has condemned and warns Israel against following the same course. The chapter is therefore concerned not only with individual conduct but also with the identity and responsibility of the people who are to live in the land. Its laws are presented as part of Israel's calling to holiness.2

Within this sequence of laws, verse 22 prohibits a male from lying with another male as with a woman. The wording identifies a particular sexual act, and the command appears among other restrictions concerning sexual relationships, including those involving close relatives and adultery. Its place in the chapter matters: the verse belongs to Leviticus's regulation of sexual conduct within Israel's covenant life. It does not refer to Sodom, Lot, the visitors in Genesis 19, or the city's destruction. The prohibition must therefore be understood first through the legal and literary setting in which Leviticus places it, rather than treated as a direct explanation of the earlier Genesis narrative.3

The term translated “abomination” appears in several places in the Hebrew Bible and does not carry an identical function in every setting. In Leviticus 18:22, the term marks the act as forbidden within the chapter's religious and legal framework. The word is important, but it cannot by itself answer every question about the nature of the prohibition, its historical application, or how later communities have interpreted it. Those questions require attention to the full passage, the laws surrounding it, and the wider themes of Leviticus. Reading the verse in context gives the term its proper weight without asking one English word to carry more meaning than the passage provides.4

Leviticus 18:22 is therefore an explicit prohibition within the covenant legislation presented to Israel. Its meaning cannot be separated from the sequence of sexual regulations in which it appears or from the chapter's larger concern for holiness and covenant identity. The chapter connects personal conduct with the life of the whole community and with Israel's responsibility in the land. Recognizing that wider setting does not weaken or remove the specific command. Rather, it helps explain where the command belongs and prevents the verse from being detached from the legal and theological framework that gives it context.

CHAPTER 13

The Law, Its Consequences, and Its Historical Setting

“If a man lies with a male as if he were a woman, both men have committed an offense (something perverse, unnatural, abhorrent, and detestable); they shall surely be put to death; their blood shall be upon them.”

Leviticus 20:13

Leviticus 20 returns to the subject addressed in chapter 18, but places the prohibition within a collection of laws that also identifies consequences for other prohibited acts. The chapter covers a range of covenant offenses, including violations within family relationships, offenses involving parents and children, adultery, prohibited sexual practices, and conduct regarded as incompatible with Israel's obligations before God. Its scope is much wider than the single issue addressed in verse 13. That verse repeats the prohibition concerning a male lying with a male and states a capital penalty. The two chapters are closely related, but they serve different functions: Leviticus 18 gathers prohibited conduct into a series of instructions, while Leviticus 20 gives attention to the consequences attached to a number of those offenses.5

The chapter's references to family offenses and prohibited sexual conduct also invite a responsible discussion of harm within the household and the protection of children. Modern language gives us direct terms for conduct that must never be minimized: child sexual abuse, grooming, coercion, and the exploitation of minors. These actions violate a child's safety, dignity, and ability to give meaningful consent, and they should be identified and condemned plainly. Leviticus 20, however, does not set out a separate modern classification of pedophilia or provide a detailed account of child sexual abuse. Its laws use the categories and language of the ancient covenant community. Modern readers can apply clear language to recognize and condemn abuse while remaining careful not to claim that the chapter explicitly defines every form of harm now identified by legal, clinical, and safeguarding frameworks.

The penalty stated in Leviticus 20:13 must also be read within the legal world represented by the Torah. The wording is severe, and a responsible account should neither conceal that severity nor assume that the written penalty tells us how often the sanction was imposed. A law's stated consequence, the procedures through which a community might apply it, and the historical evidence for actual enforcement are separate questions. Leviticus records the penalty as part of Israel's legislation, but the passage does not provide case records, describe particular court proceedings, or tell us how frequently the sanction was carried out. The distinction between the law as written and its historical application is essential when interpreting ancient legal material.6

The chapter's closing section helps place these individual laws within the larger purpose of Leviticus. In verses 22–26, Israel is again called to keep the statutes and to distinguish its conduct from the practices attributed to the peoples of the land. The instructions are connected with the people's covenant identity and their responsibility to live as a community belonging to the LORD. Leviticus 20 is thus not simply a list of private rules and penalties. In the book's own framework, conduct has consequences for communal life and reflects the people's relationship with God. This larger setting explains why the chapter gathers different offenses under the shared concern for holiness.7

Questions about the history of Leviticus require a different kind of evidence from questions about what the final text says. Scholars continue to discuss when the book's laws took shape, how its sections were arranged, and how the traditions developed before reaching the form preserved in the Torah. Those debates matter for understanding the history of Israel's law, but they should not obscure the text in front of the reader. In its final form, Leviticus presents these instructions as part of the covenant teaching given to Israel and places them within the broader call to holiness. Historical reconstruction can investigate how that material emerged and functioned; close reading first establishes how the book itself presents it.8

Modern readers also bring words and categories to these passages that did not exist in the same form in the ancient world. Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 describe a specific sexual act using the language of a male lying with another male as with a woman. They do not offer a modern account of sexual orientation, personal identity, or contemporary relationships. This distinction is important in both directions. The wording should not be softened or ignored, but neither should the verses be made to address every modern question about identity or relationships when those subjects are not discussed in the text. Responsible interpretation begins with the act the passage names, considers its place in Israel's law, and clearly distinguishes the ancient wording from later applications and debates.9

Taken together, Leviticus 18 and 20 show that the prohibition appears in two related parts of Israel's legal tradition: chapter 18 identifies the act as prohibited, and chapter 20 repeats the prohibition within a collection that states consequences for various offenses. The passages provide clear evidence that this conduct was restricted in the covenant legislation represented by Leviticus. They also show why context matters. The command belongs to a wider body of laws concerned with holiness, family and community life, and Israel's covenant obligations. A careful reading takes the prohibition and its stated penalty seriously while distinguishing what the text says from questions it leaves unanswered.

CHAPTER 14

The Difference Between Sodom and Israel's Law

“But the men of Sodom were wicked and exceedingly great sinners against the Lord.”

Genesis 13:13

Having examined the laws themselves, the study can now return to the question that connects this part with the earlier account of Sodom. Later discussions often place Genesis 19 beside Leviticus 18 and 20, especially when considering sexual conduct. The passages can be compared, but they do not tell the same kind of story. Genesis 19 is a narrative describing the visitors who arrive at Lot's house, the crowd's threat of sexual violence, Lot's response, the rescue of his household, and the city's destruction. Leviticus, by contrast, presents laws for Israel's covenant community. One passage recounts events; the others set out expectations and legal consequences. That difference in purpose, audience, and literary form must guide any comparison.10

The chronology reinforces this distinction. In the biblical sequence, the events involving Abraham and Lot take place long before the exodus and the covenant at Sinai, within which Leviticus presents its laws. Genesis does not say that Sodom's inhabitants were living under Israel's later legislation, and Leviticus does not claim that its laws served as the legal code by which the city was judged. The two passages therefore cannot be collapsed into a single legal setting. Any claim that the Levitical prohibitions directly explain the destruction of Sodom would go beyond the statements made in Genesis and Leviticus themselves.11

Reading the passages together is still meaningful. Jewish and Christian interpreters have drawn on Genesis, Leviticus, and other biblical writings when developing broader teachings about sexual conduct, holiness, and divine judgment. Those connections belong to the history of biblical interpretation and can be studied on their own terms. The important point is to identify them accurately. A later reader may connect the laws of Leviticus with the story of Sodom, but Genesis does not quote these laws or say that they were the reason for the city's destruction. The distinction between the wording of the original passages and the conclusions drawn by later interpreters should remain visible.12

Ezekiel's earlier use of Sodom provides another point of comparison. In Ezekiel 16:49–50, the prophet recalls the city's pride, abundance, and failure to support the poor and needy, and also refers to haughtiness and abomination. He uses this memory to confront Jerusalem and expose the failures of God's people. That prophetic passage does not erase the events narrated in Genesis, nor does it make Leviticus an explanation of Sodom's destruction. Instead, it shows that biblical writers could draw on Sodom's memory for distinct purposes. Genesis tells the story of the city, Ezekiel turns its memory into a warning for Jerusalem, and Leviticus sets out covenant laws for Israel. Each text contributes a different kind of evidence to the wider study.

These distinctions help avoid two mistakes. The first is to overlook the direct prohibitions in Leviticus 18 and 20 when describing the laws that the Torah presents for Israel. The second is to treat those laws as the stated legal reason for an earlier event when Genesis does not make that connection. Careful interpretation does not require choosing one passage and ignoring the others. It requires allowing each passage to speak within its own setting, then explaining how later readers have brought them together and what those connections can and cannot establish.

Leviticus adds an essential part of the biblical evidence, but its contribution is different from that of Genesis and Ezekiel. It records the standards of conduct presented for Israel, including explicit restrictions concerning male same-sex intercourse and a stated penalty in chapter 20. It also places those instructions within a broader collection of laws concerning family relationships, sexual conduct, communal responsibility, and holiness. These laws may be studied alongside the narrative of Sodom and the later prophetic use of its memory, provided their distinct purposes are respected. The connection belongs to the wider biblical and interpretive tradition; the passages themselves are not interchangeable.

WHAT LEVITICUS ADDS TO THE JOURNEY

Leviticus has brought the study from prophetic memory to the laws governing Israel's covenant life. Chapters 18 and 20 contain explicit prohibitions concerning male same-sex intercourse, and chapter 20 records a penalty within a wider collection of legal consequences. Their surrounding instructions address a broader range of conduct and responsibilities, including family relationships, sexual behavior, communal order, and holiness. Reading these chapters carefully means taking their wording seriously while distinguishing the law as written from the separate historical question of how it was applied.

Placed beside the earlier texts, Leviticus contributes legislation rather than another account of Sodom's destruction. Genesis narrates the city's fate, Ezekiel uses its memory to challenge Jerusalem, and Leviticus describes the covenant standards given to Israel. The next part turns to Isaiah, where Sodom appears again within a prophetic message directed toward God's people. There the emphasis falls on the condition of Judah, the integrity of worship, and the demands of justice.
