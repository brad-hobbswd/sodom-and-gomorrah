PART FOUR

SODOM AND ISRAEL'S LAWS

Leviticus shifts the discussion from the story of Sodom to the laws governing ancient Israel. This distinction matters because Genesis and Leviticus serve different purposes. Genesis recounts the events surrounding Sodom's destruction, while Leviticus sets out standards of conduct for Israel's covenant community.

To understand how these passages relate, we must first consider what each text says in its own setting rather than treating them as though they were written to explain the same event.

CHAPTER 8

Leviticus and the Holiness of Israel

“You shall not lie with a male, as with a woman. It is an abomination.”

Leviticus 18:22

Leviticus explains how Israel was expected to live as a people set apart for God. Its laws address worship, sacrifices, ritual purity, family relationships, sexual conduct, and responsibilities within the community. These instructions belong to Israel's covenant tradition and reflect the holiness standards through which the nation was expected to order its life before God.

Within this collection, Leviticus 18:22 prohibits male same sex intercourse. The subject appears again in Leviticus 20, where the prohibition is placed within Israel's legal system and a penalty is specified.

“If a man lies with a male, as with a woman, both of them have committed an abomination. They shall surely be put to death. Their blood shall be upon them.”

Leviticus 20:13

Read together, these passages show that Israel's covenant legislation expressly addressed sexual relations between males. Leviticus 18 presents the prohibition among laws governing sexual conduct, while Leviticus 20 restates it in a section addressing the consequences of prohibited behavior.

The historical significance is clear: these laws provide evidence of how sexual conduct was regulated within ancient Israel's covenant and legal framework. Their significance, however, must be distinguished from the earlier narrative of Sodom.

The Difference Between Sodom and Israel's Law

Genesis 19 tells the story of Sodom's destruction. It describes the arrival of the visitors, the response of the city's men, Lot's actions, the threat of sexual violence, and the judgment that follows. Leviticus, by contrast, presents laws for the people of Israel. It does not state that Sodom was destroyed for violating the specific prohibitions later recorded in Israel's legal code.

The events in Genesis precede the establishment of Israel's covenant law at Sinai. We therefore should not assume that the people of Sodom were being judged under the same legal framework later given to Israel. Genesis must be read within its own narrative, and Leviticus within its covenant setting.

Nevertheless, the passages became connected in later Jewish and Christian interpretation because both were considered in discussions of sexual morality and divine judgment. That connection is worth examining, but it does not make the passages identical in purpose. Leviticus can inform our understanding of Israel's moral and religious traditions without serving as a direct explanation for why Sodom was destroyed.

This is the essential distinction: two passages can address related subjects without telling the same story or answering the same question.

What Leviticus Adds to the Study of Sodom

“Now the men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinners against the LORD.”

Genesis 13:13

Leviticus contributes one part of the wider biblical picture. It records Israel's covenant laws concerning sexual conduct and places them within a broader system of holiness, covenant identity, and community responsibility. Other passages contribute different perspectives. Genesis recounts Sodom's destruction, while Ezekiel 16:49 emphasizes the city's pride, abundance, complacency, and failure to help the poor and needy.

These texts can be considered together, but each must retain its own historical and literary context. Genesis does not present the later laws of Leviticus as the legal basis for Sodom's destruction, and Leviticus does not identify itself as an explanation of that event.

Reading carefully allows us to recognize both the connections and the differences within the biblical tradition. It avoids making Leviticus explain an event that its text does not directly address, while acknowledging the role its laws played in later discussions of sexuality and morality.

The goal is not to force every passage into a single explanation. It is to understand what each passage says, what it contributes, and how its meaning has been considered alongside other biblical texts.

WHAT LEVITICUS ADDS TO THE JOURNEY

Leviticus records restrictions concerning male same sex intercourse within Israel's covenant legislation. Genesis tells the story of Sodom's destruction without identifying those later laws as the direct legal basis for its judgment.

Genesis, Ezekiel, and Leviticus therefore contribute different kinds of evidence. Genesis tells the story of Sodom, Ezekiel reflects on the city's wrongdoing, and Leviticus records Israel's covenant laws. Understanding their relationship begins by allowing each text to speak within its own setting.
