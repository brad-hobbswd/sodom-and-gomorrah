PART FOUR

SODOM AND ISRAEL'S LAWS

The biblical memory of Sodom now brings the study to a different kind of text. Genesis narrated the city's destruction, and Ezekiel later recalled Sodom while confronting Jerusalem with the consequences of pride, prosperity, and neglect. Leviticus does not continue either account as a narrative. Instead, the book presents instructions for Israel's life as a covenant people, including laws concerning worship, family relationships, sexual conduct, justice, and the treatment of others. The change in literary form is important, since a story about a city and a body of legislation do not answer the same historical or theological questions.

This part examines Leviticus 18 and 20, with particular attention to the prohibitions recorded in those chapters and the place they occupy within Israel's holiness legislation. The aim is to understand the wording, surrounding context, and legal function of the passages before considering how later readers connected them with Sodom. Such a reading requires care: Leviticus does address male same-sex intercourse in its own covenant setting, but the text does not identify those laws as the legal explanation for the destruction narrated in Genesis. Keeping those distinctions visible allows the passages to be compared without making one say what the other does not.

CHAPTER 12

Leviticus and the Holiness of Israel

“You shall not lie with a man as with a woman; it is an abomination.”

Leviticus 18:22

Leviticus stands within the Torah as a collection of instructions concerned with Israel's worship and communal life. Its material includes offerings, priestly responsibilities, ritual purity, sacred times, ethical obligations, and rules governing relationships within the community. The book repeatedly connects Israel's conduct with the holiness of the God whom the people worship. Its concern is therefore broader than a list of isolated prohibitions: the instructions describe a people being taught how to order communal life in response to their covenant relationship with God.1

Leviticus 18 belongs to a section commonly identified as part of the Holiness legislation, especially because the chapter repeatedly grounds its commands in the declaration that the people are to be holy before the LORD. The chapter begins by warning Israel not to imitate the practices associated with Egypt, where the people had lived, or Canaan, the land toward which they were going. It then sets out a series of prohibited sexual relationships and practices, before explaining that the land itself is portrayed as defiled by such conduct and that Israel must not follow the same pattern. The chapter's stated concern is the moral and covenant identity of the community living in the land.2

Within this collection, verse 22 states, “You shall not lie with a male, as with a woman. It is an abomination.” The wording identifies a prohibited act involving a male lying with another male in the manner described by the verse. The command appears among restrictions concerning sexual relations, including prohibitions involving close relatives and adultery. Its immediate literary setting is therefore the chapter's wider regulation of sexual conduct, not the account of Sodom in Genesis 19. The verse does not mention Sodom, Lot, the visitors, or the destruction of the city.3

The word translated “abomination” is also used elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible in a range of contexts. Its presence signals that the conduct is prohibited within the law's religious and moral framework, but the word alone does not explain every dimension of the offense or provide a complete account of how the command was understood in every period. Interpretation must therefore attend to the particular command, its surrounding laws, and the larger argument of Leviticus rather than relying on a single English term to settle every question.4

These observations establish what the passage directly contributes to the study. Leviticus 18:22 is an explicit prohibition within Israel's covenant legislation, and its position among laws governing sexual conduct is central to understanding its function. At the same time, the chapter's wider concerns include holiness, communal boundaries, the land, and Israel's distinction from the practices attributed to surrounding peoples. A careful reading considers the specific prohibition without detaching it from the literary and theological framework in which the law appears.

CHAPTER 13

The Law, Its Consequences, and Its Historical Setting

“If a man lies with a male as if he were a woman, both men have committed an offense (something perverse, unnatural, abhorrent, and detestable); they shall surely be put to death; their blood shall be upon them.”

Leviticus 20:13

Leviticus 20 returns to the prohibition found in chapter 18, but places the instruction within a section that identifies consequences for a range of prohibited acts. The chapter addresses offenses involving family relationships, adultery, prohibited sexual practices, and other conduct regarded as incompatible with Israel's covenant obligations. Verse 13 repeats the prohibition concerning a male lying with a male and states a capital penalty. The relationship between the two passages is therefore close, although their immediate functions differ: Leviticus 18 presents the prohibition within a catalogue of prohibited conduct, while Leviticus 20 places the corresponding offense within a collection of legal sanctions.5

The penalty recorded in Leviticus 20:13 belongs to the legal world represented by the Torah. Readers should neither omit the severity of the wording nor assume that a biblical legal prescription tells us, by itself, how frequently a penalty was imposed in practice. A law's written sanction, the procedures by which a community might apply it, and the historical record of actual enforcement are related but distinct matters. The passage states the penalty within Israel's law; it does not provide a narrative record of individual cases, courts, or the frequency with which the sanction was carried out.6

The surrounding material also matters. Leviticus 20 does not isolate verse 13 as a stand-alone discussion. The chapter groups a number of offenses and consequences within its larger concern for the holiness of the people. It repeatedly calls Israel to distinguish its conduct from practices that the text associates with the peoples of the land. The chapter thus connects legal instruction with covenant identity, communal order, and the claim that Israel belongs to the LORD. This setting helps explain why the law is presented as more than a private rule: within the text's own framework, the conduct of the community has religious and social significance.7

Historical interpretation must also recognize the limits of what can be reconstructed. Leviticus preserves Israel's religious and legal traditions, and scholars differ over questions concerning the formation, arrangement, and dating of the book's materials. The final form of Leviticus presents these laws as part of the covenant instruction given to Israel, while historical study investigates how these traditions developed and functioned over time. Those questions should be distinguished from the plain observation that the text itself places the prohibitions within Israel's holiness legislation.8

Another important distinction concerns the language of the ancient text and the categories used in modern discussion. Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 describe a particular act in terms of one male lying with another male as with a woman. The verses do not set out a modern taxonomy of sexual orientation or discuss contemporary concepts of identity, partnership, or personal experience. Readers may debate how the commands should be interpreted and applied, but historical explanation should first identify what the ancient wording addresses and avoid claiming that the text explicitly answers questions it does not discuss. This distinction does not erase the force of the prohibition within Leviticus; it keeps interpretation accountable to the scope of the passage.9

Read together, chapters 18 and 20 show that the prohibition is not an incidental phrase appearing only once. It is included in two related legal collections, first as a prohibited act and again with a stated penalty. The passages therefore provide clear evidence that this conduct was restricted within the covenant legislation represented by Leviticus. Understanding that evidence requires neither minimizing the text nor extending its wording beyond the questions it actually addresses.

CHAPTER 14

The Difference Between Sodom and Israel's Law

“But the men of Sodom were wicked and exceedingly great sinners against the Lord.”

Genesis 13:13

The connection between Sodom and Leviticus is often made in later discussions of sexual morality, but the biblical passages themselves must first be read in sequence and in context. Genesis 19 is a narrative about the arrival of visitors, Lot's response, the actions of the men gathered at his house, the threat of sexual violence, the rescue of Lot's household, and the destruction of the city. Leviticus 18 and 20 are laws addressed to Israel's covenant community. Genesis recounts an event; Leviticus sets out instructions and sanctions. The difference in genre and audience is essential to a responsible comparison.10

The chronology also prevents a simple identification of the two settings. In the biblical narrative, the events involving Abraham and Lot occur before the exodus from Egypt and the covenant legislation associated with Sinai. Leviticus presents laws for Israel as a people formed through that covenant. Genesis does not say that the inhabitants of Sodom had received the later legislation of Leviticus, and Leviticus does not state that its prohibitions were the legal code under which Sodom was judged. To claim that Genesis directly identifies the Levitical statutes as the cause of Sodom's destruction would go beyond what either passage says.11

This distinction does not mean that the passages can never be considered together. Later Jewish and Christian readers have brought Genesis, Leviticus, and other biblical texts into conversation when forming broader accounts of sexual conduct, holiness, and judgment. Such interpretation is part of the history of how the Bible has been read. However, a later theological connection should be identified as an interpretive development rather than presented as though Genesis itself quotes Leviticus or explains Sodom's fate through the wording of chapters 18 and 20.12

The earlier prophetic witness adds another dimension. Ezekiel 16:49–50 recalls Sodom through the language of pride, fullness of food, prosperous ease, and failure to strengthen the poor and needy, before also referring to haughtiness and abomination. Ezekiel's purpose is to confront Jerusalem through a comparison with Samaria and Sodom. His passage does not cancel Genesis 19, and it does not turn Leviticus into a commentary on the destruction. Instead, Ezekiel demonstrates how the memory of Sodom could be selected and applied within a later prophetic argument. The three passages contribute different kinds of material: Genesis narrates, Ezekiel interprets the memory of Sodom, and Leviticus legislates for Israel.

Keeping these distinctions in view helps prevent two opposite errors. One would be to ignore the explicit content of Leviticus 18 and 20 when discussing Israel's covenant laws. The other would be to use those laws as a direct explanation for an earlier narrative that does not identify them as its legal basis. Historical and literary interpretation is strongest when it allows each text to retain its own voice, while still recognizing the ways later readers have related the passages to one another.

Leviticus therefore adds an important but distinct element to this study. It documents the standards of conduct that the Torah presents for Israel, including explicit restrictions concerning male same-sex intercourse and a stated penalty in chapter 20. Those laws belong to the covenant framework of ancient Israel. They can be examined alongside the story and later memory of Sodom, but the relationship must be described accurately: the texts are connected within the wider biblical and interpretive tradition without being identical in purpose, setting, or subject.

WHAT LEVITICUS ADDS TO THE JOURNEY

Leviticus has brought the study from prophetic memory to covenant legislation. Chapters 18 and 20 expressly address male same-sex intercourse within Israel's legal and holiness framework, while the surrounding material connects those instructions with the identity and responsibilities of the covenant community. The text also requires attention to the distinction between a prohibition, its stated sanction, and the historical question of how laws were applied in practice.

Read beside Genesis and Ezekiel, Leviticus adds a different kind of evidence rather than a replacement explanation for Sodom's destruction. Genesis tells the story of the city, Ezekiel uses its memory to confront Jerusalem, and Leviticus records laws for Israel. The next part turns to Isaiah and the prophetic tradition, where Sodom appears again as a comparison in a message about the condition of God's people and the demands of justice.
