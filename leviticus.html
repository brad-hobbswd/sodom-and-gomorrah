PART FOUR

SODOM AND ISRAEL'S LAWS

By the time we reach Leviticus, the biblical tradition has moved into a very different setting. We are no longer reading the story of Sodom itself. We are reading laws given to Israel and preserved within Israel's covenant tradition.

That difference matters. If we want to understand what the Bible says about Sodom, sexuality, and morality, we must first identify which text we are reading and what that text is trying to accomplish.

CHAPTER 8

Leviticus and the Holiness of Israel

Leviticus belongs to a different part of Israel's history and to a different kind of biblical literature.

The book is deeply concerned with holiness. Its laws address worship, sacrifices, ritual purity, family relationships, sexuality, community responsibilities, and the ways Israel was expected to live as a people belonging to God.

This matters when we begin examining what Leviticus says about sexual behavior.

Leviticus 18:22 addresses sexual relations between males. The passage places the prohibition within a larger collection of laws concerning sexual relationships and the boundaries Israel was expected to observe.

Leviticus 20:13 addresses the subject again. This time, the passage places the prohibition within Israel's legal system and describes a specific penalty.

These passages are historically important because they demonstrate that Israel's covenant laws included specific restrictions concerning male same sex intercourse.

At the same time, these passages should not simply be inserted into the story of Sodom as though they were written to explain why Sodom was destroyed.

They were not.

Genesis and Leviticus come from different literary settings and serve different purposes within the biblical tradition. Genesis 19 tells a narrative about Sodom, its inhabitants, Lot, the visiting messengers, violence, judgment, and destruction. Leviticus presents laws governing Israel's covenant community.

That distinction is essential.

A historical reading does not require every biblical passage that discusses sexuality to be treated as though it is describing the same event or answering the same question.

The Difference Between Sodom and Israel's Law

This distinction also helps us avoid an important mistake.

Leviticus 18 and 20 do not say that Sodom was destroyed because the people of Sodom violated the laws of Leviticus. The laws of Leviticus were given to Israel within the framework of Israel's covenant and holiness system. The destruction of Sodom is narrated in Genesis as part of an earlier story.

That does not mean the passages have nothing to do with one another.

Later readers naturally placed them alongside one another because both traditions discuss sexual behavior. They became part of a much larger history of Jewish and Christian interpretation concerning sexuality, holiness, morality, and the relationship between human behavior and divine judgment.

But historical interpretation requires us to preserve the difference between connection and identity.

Two passages can address related subjects without being the same story.

This principle is especially important when studying ancient texts. A later law can help us understand the moral world of the community that preserved it without becoming a missing explanation for an earlier narrative.

What Leviticus Actually Adds

Leviticus therefore adds something important to our study.

Genesis tells us what happened in the story of Sodom.

Ezekiel tells us how Israel later remembered Sodom, particularly emphasizing pride, prosperity, arrogance, and failure to help the poor and needy.

Leviticus gives us something different: evidence of Israel's own covenant legislation concerning sexual behavior.

These traditions belong beside one another, but they should not be collapsed into one another.

Leviticus also reminds us that biblical discussions of sexuality did not exist in isolation. They were connected to Israel's understanding of holiness, covenant identity, family life, worship, and the boundaries that distinguished Israel from surrounding peoples.

That broader setting helps us read the laws as ancient Israelite legislation rather than as isolated statements removed from their historical world.

The Bible's treatment of sexuality therefore cannot be understood responsibly by taking one verse out of its surrounding collection of laws, just as the story of Sodom cannot be understood responsibly by taking one scene from Genesis 19 and ignoring the rest of the biblical tradition.

WHAT LEVITICUS ADDS TO THE JOURNEY

Leviticus provides clear evidence that Israel's covenant laws included restrictions concerning male same sex intercourse. At the same time, those laws belong to Israel's holiness and legal tradition rather than serving as a direct explanation for the destruction of Sodom.

Genesis, Ezekiel, and Leviticus each contribute different evidence. The historical task is not to make them say the same thing, but to understand how their individual voices became part of the larger biblical memory of Sodom, morality, holiness, and judgment.
