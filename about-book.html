ABOUT THE BOOK

Sodom and Gomorrah

A historical and biblical study of an ancient city, the texts that remember it, and the meanings that developed across generations.

The account of Sodom and Gomorrah has occupied a lasting place in religious thought, biblical interpretation, and historical discussion. Few ancient narratives have been remembered in so many different ways or brought into so many debates about human conduct, justice, sexuality, violence, hospitality, and divine judgment. This study examines that long history by returning to the sources and asking what each text actually says, what its original setting contributes to its meaning, and where the available evidence leaves questions unresolved.

THE PURPOSE

Read the Sources in Their Own Context

This book is designed to move beyond a single verse, a familiar slogan, or one inherited conclusion. The biblical references to Sodom were written in different settings and serve different purposes. Genesis presents the foundational narrative. The prophets recall Sodom while addressing the conduct and responsibilities of their own communities. Israel's legal material belongs to a distinct covenant setting. The New Testament writers draw upon the city's memory in ways shaped by their individual arguments.

Reading these passages together is valuable, but doing so responsibly requires attention to their differences. A later reference should not automatically be treated as though it repeats every detail of the Genesis account. Each passage deserves to be heard within its own literary, historical, and theological setting before its relationship to the wider biblical record is considered.

THE STRUCTURE

A Journey Through Nine Parts

The study is arranged as a progression. It begins with the ancient world associated with Abraham and Lot, then turns to the Genesis narrative and the later biblical texts that remember Sodom. The journey continues through prophetic interpretation, Israel's law, the teachings of Jesus, the writings of Peter and Jude, and a carefully qualified comparison involving David and Jonathan. The final sections trace the development of Sodom's symbolic meaning and assess what the historical record can and cannot establish.

This arrangement allows the reader to follow the evidence in sequence. Historical background is introduced before it is used to interpret the biblical narrative. Later biblical references are examined in their own contexts rather than blended into one undifferentiated account. The final historical assessment brings the strands together without claiming more certainty than the sources permit.

THE RESEARCH APPROACH

Evidence, Interpretation, and Historical Limits

Throughout the book, a distinction is maintained between the wording of a biblical passage, the historical background that may illuminate it, and the interpretation offered by readers or scholars. These categories can inform one another, but they are not interchangeable. An interpretation may be persuasive without being an explicit statement of the text. A historical parallel may provide context without proving that the biblical writer had that particular example in mind.

The study also recognizes the limits of archaeological certainty. Proposed locations for Sodom and Gomorrah, evidence of ancient settlement, and theories about the cities' destruction remain subjects of discussion. The available evidence does not justify presenting one proposed location or physical explanation as conclusively established. Where certainty is unavailable, the book identifies the question rather than disguising uncertainty as fact.

A CAREFUL READING

Ancient Texts and Modern Questions

Readers today often approach ancient passages with questions shaped by modern language, social categories, legal standards, and ethical concerns. Those questions deserve serious attention. At the same time, modern terms do not always correspond directly to the categories used by ancient writers. This book seeks to make those differences clear, to identify harm and injustice plainly where the texts support such discussion, and to avoid assigning ancient passages meanings they do not explicitly establish.

The goal is not to avoid difficult subjects. The goal is to address them with accuracy, care, and intellectual honesty. Scripture, historical context, and contemporary application each have a place in the discussion, provided their distinct roles remain clear.

WHO THIS BOOK SERVES

For Readers Who Want to Examine the Record

This study is intended for readers interested in biblical history, theological interpretation, ancient culture, and the ways scriptural narratives shape later thought. It may be useful for personal study, classroom discussion, small-group learning, or readers who want to examine the sources before reaching a conclusion of their own.

No reader is asked to set aside difficult questions. Instead, readers are invited to move carefully through the material, consider the evidence, recognize the limits of what can be known, and allow each source to contribute what it can genuinely support.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR

Bradley Hobbs

Bradley Hobbs prepared this study with an interest in biblical interpretation, historical context, and careful examination of the texts that have shaped religious understanding.
