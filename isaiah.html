PART FIVE

SODOM IN THE PROPHETS

As Israel's history continued, the memory of Sodom moved beyond the original Genesis narrative. The prophets could use the name of Sodom and Gomorrah when speaking to their own generation about injustice, religious hypocrisy, corruption, judgment, and the consequences of turning away from God.

Isaiah is especially important because he does not simply retell the story of Genesis 19. He takes a name already loaded with historical memory and uses it to confront Judah in its own circumstances.

CHAPTER 9

Isaiah and the Political Use of Sodom

Isaiah uses Sodom and Gomorrah as symbols when confronting Judah.

This happened during a period when the people of Judah were experiencing political and religious crisis. Isaiah's message repeatedly challenged the gap between outward religious activity and the actual condition of society.

In Isaiah 1, the prophet addresses people who continue religious practices while failing to practice justice. The problem is not presented as a lack of religious activity. The problem is that religious activity has become disconnected from righteous behavior.

Isaiah calls the people to learn to do good, seek justice, correct oppression, defend the vulnerable, and care for those who have been wronged.

It is within this confrontation that Isaiah invokes Sodom and Gomorrah.

Sodom therefore becomes more than an ancient city.

It becomes a symbol.

To compare Judah with Sodom is to place Judah under the shadow of a remembered judgment. The comparison warns that a society cannot claim faithfulness to God while practicing injustice and ignoring righteousness.

This is how historical memory works.

A past event becomes a warning for a later generation.

Isaiah is therefore not giving us another complete account of everything that happened at Sodom. He is using Sodom rhetorically. The name carries the weight of the earlier story, allowing Isaiah to confront his own society with a familiar example of destruction and moral failure.

This distinction is important. When a prophet invokes Sodom, we should ask what aspect of Sodom's memory the prophet is emphasizing in that particular passage rather than assuming that every reference is intended to repeat the entire Genesis account.

CHAPTER 10

Sodom Becomes a Symbol

As Israel's history continued, Sodom became part of the nation's moral vocabulary.

It could represent wickedness.

It could represent arrogance.

It could represent injustice.

It could represent judgment.

It could represent rebellion against God.

The power of the name came from the accumulated memory of the biblical tradition. Sodom was no longer remembered only as a geographical location or as the setting of one ancient story. It became a shorthand for a society that had reached a serious state of moral corruption and stood under divine judgment.

That does not mean every later writer meant exactly the same thing when using the name.

One writer might emphasize pride. Another might emphasize injustice. Another might emphasize sexual wrongdoing. Another might emphasize rebellion or judgment.

Different writers could emphasize different aspects because the name itself had become associated with complete moral collapse.

This development is important for understanding the rest of the biblical tradition. When Jesus, Peter, Jude, or other later writers refer to Sodom, they are not necessarily beginning the story again. They are entering a conversation that has already been developing for generations.

The memory of Sodom had become layered.

Genesis provided the foundational narrative. Ezekiel emphasized pride, prosperity, and failure to help the vulnerable. Leviticus provided Israel's own covenant laws concerning sexuality. Isaiah used Sodom to confront religious hypocrisy and injustice.

The result is not a single sentence defining Sodom.

It is a developing historical memory.

WHAT ISAIAH ADDS TO THE JOURNEY

Isaiah shows how Sodom could be taken from the memory of an ancient city and applied to the moral and political circumstances of a later generation. The prophet's use of Sodom demonstrates that biblical writers could invoke the city's name to challenge injustice, religious hypocrisy, and rebellion against God.

By this point in the biblical tradition, Sodom has become more than a place on a map. It has become a warning carried from one generation to another.
