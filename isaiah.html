PART FIVE

SODOM IN THE PROPHETS

The study has now moved through several important stages in the biblical record. Genesis introduced the city and the events surrounding its destruction. Ezekiel later recalled Sodom while confronting Jerusalem with the consequences of pride, prosperity, and neglect of those in need. Leviticus brought the discussion into Israel's covenant legislation, establishing a different kind of biblical witness through its instructions concerning holiness and the responsibilities of the covenant community. Each part has contributed evidence from a distinct literary and historical setting, and the differences between those settings remain essential to understanding the larger subject.

The prophetic tradition now takes the study in another direction. Rather than returning to the original account or presenting another collection of covenant laws, Isaiah draws upon the memory of Sodom to address the spiritual and moral condition of Judah. His words reveal how the name of an ancient city could become a warning to a later generation, particularly when religious observance existed alongside injustice and the mistreatment of vulnerable people. Isaiah is not simply retelling the destruction recorded in Genesis; he is confronting his own society through a remembered example whose force his audience would have recognized.

Understanding this comparison requires attention to the circumstances of Judah, the purpose of Isaiah's message, and the relationship between worship and righteous living. The prophet's language is severe, yet his argument is not limited to denunciation. He calls the people toward a different way of life, one in which worship is joined to justice, leadership is measured by responsibility, and the vulnerable are no longer abandoned. Through Isaiah, Sodom becomes part of a larger prophetic conversation about accountability and the conduct expected of a people who claim to belong to God.

CHAPTER 15

Isaiah and the Political and Religious Condition of Judah

“Hear [O Jerusalem] the word of the Lord, you rulers or judges of [another] Sodom! Give ear to the law and the teaching of our God, you people of [another] Gomorrah!”

Isaiah 1:10

Isaiah's opening chapter places the reader within a confrontation between God and Judah. The book's superscription identifies the prophet's ministry with the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah whose periods of rule spanned significant political and religious changes. The prophetic collection reflects a world in which the kingdom faced questions of leadership, security, worship, national loyalty, and the consequences of injustice. Although the book brings together material from a complex history of composition and transmission, its opening vision presents a clear challenge: Judah's relationship with God cannot be measured by religious claims alone when the life of the community contradicts the justice that the covenant requires.1

Isaiah 1 begins with the language of a rebellious people. The prophet describes a community that has failed to recognize the one who sustains it, even though the people continue to occupy the place of God's chosen nation. The language of sickness and injury portrays a society whose condition is damaged from head to foot. The imagery is not offered as a medical description or a report of one isolated political event. It is a prophetic diagnosis of collective disorder, in which rebellion has affected the life of the nation and left its condition exposed before God.2

Within this setting, Isaiah addresses the rulers as the rulers of Sodom and the people as the people of Gomorrah. The prophet is not identifying the residents of Jerusalem as the literal inhabitants of the ancient cities, nor is he suggesting that Judah has become geographically identical with Sodom. The names function as an accusation. By applying the remembered names of cities associated with judgment to Judah's leaders and people, Isaiah places the present generation under the moral weight of a familiar biblical warning. The comparison is deliberately forceful because the people who consider themselves recipients of God's instruction are being confronted as though they have repeated the failures associated with a notorious example of rebellion.3

The next verses make the accusation more pointed. Isaiah questions the value of offerings, assemblies, festivals, and prayers when the people's conduct remains corrupt. The prophet does not argue that worship, sacrifice, or sacred gatherings are inherently wrong. His criticism is directed toward religious practice that has become detached from obedience and justice. The people continue to approach God through outward observance while the society around them bears the consequences of wrongdoing. Their ceremonies cannot conceal the condition of their public life, and the abundance of religious activity does not excuse the absence of righteousness.4

This is the setting in which Sodom enters Isaiah's message. The name carries the memory of a city judged for its wickedness, but Isaiah's immediate purpose is to confront Judah. His comparison asks the people to consider whether their confidence in religious identity has blinded them to the condition of their own community. The warning is therefore directed toward those who possess the language and practices of faith while failing to embody the responsibilities that accompany them. Isaiah turns the remembered past into a mirror held before the present.

Reading the passage in this way also guards against treating every biblical reference to Sodom as though each writer were making precisely the same argument. Genesis 19 gives the narrative of the city's destruction. Ezekiel 16 uses Sodom in a comparison that emphasizes pride, abundance, and neglect of the poor and needy. Isaiah 1 addresses Judah's rulers and people through the language of Sodom and Gomorrah while moving directly into a critique of worship disconnected from justice. The references belong to a developing biblical memory, but their immediate purposes must be understood from the passages in which they appear.

Isaiah's opening comparison is thus not an invitation to abandon historical context in favor of a single universal label. It is a carefully placed prophetic accusation against a particular community. The people of Judah are being called to account for the distance between their religious profession and their social conduct. The next movement of the chapter explains what genuine change must look like, shifting the emphasis from accusation toward the practical demands of justice.

CHAPTER 16

Sodom, Religious Hypocrisy, and the Demand for Justice

“Learn to do right! Seek justice, relieve the oppressed, and correct the oppressor. Defend the fatherless, plead for the widow.”

Isaiah 1:17

Isaiah's criticism of Judah's worship reaches its purpose in the commands that follow. The prophet calls the people to wash themselves, put away evil, cease doing wrong, learn to do good, seek justice, correct oppression, defend the fatherless, and plead the cause of the widow. These instructions are not detached moral sayings placed beside the discussion of worship. They explain the kind of life that must accompany the people's relationship with God. The prophet's concern is that religious observance has lost its integrity because the community has failed to practice the justice that should shape its public and private conduct.5

The order of Isaiah's argument deserves attention. The prophet first exposes the contradiction between religious activity and wrongdoing, then calls for a change in conduct. The answer to hollow worship is not a more impressive ceremony or a larger display of devotion. The people must turn away from evil and learn to act justly. In this passage, repentance is presented as a change that becomes visible in the treatment of others, especially those whose position leaves them vulnerable to exploitation or neglect.

The repeated concern for the oppressed, the fatherless, and the widow reflects the responsibilities that appear throughout Israel's Scriptures. Those without strong family protection or economic security could be especially exposed to abuse, dispossession, and the failure of public institutions. The commands in Isaiah therefore address more than private kindness. They call those with authority and influence to use their position to correct injustice and protect people who might otherwise be ignored. The prophet's vision of righteousness includes the way a society organizes its common life and responds to those who have little power to defend themselves.6

Isaiah's use of Sodom intensifies this appeal. The name is not introduced as a decorative insult; it establishes the seriousness of the prophet's charge. Judah cannot appeal to its religious traditions as proof of faithfulness while continuing practices that contradict the justice those traditions demand. The comparison places the people within the shadow of a remembered judgment, while the commands that follow show that the prophet is calling them to change before the consequences of their conduct are complete. The warning and the invitation belong together: the people are exposed for their wrongdoing, yet they are also summoned to return to a life shaped by righteousness.

The passage then moves toward an invitation to reason together, followed by a contrast between willing obedience and continued rebellion. Isaiah 1:18–20 uses vivid language to describe the possibility of cleansing and the seriousness of refusal. The movement from accusation to invitation demonstrates that the prophet's message is not simply a declaration that Judah is beyond hope. The people are being confronted with the reality of their condition and called to respond. The possibility of restoration does not make the warning less serious; rather, it shows why the warning is being delivered.7

Later in the same chapter, Isaiah returns to the condition of the city and describes how justice and righteousness have been displaced by corruption. The faithful city has become unfaithful; those responsible for judgment have become associated with wrongdoing, and the vulnerable are not receiving the protection they deserve. The prophet's language connects the failure of leadership with the wider moral condition of the community. A city cannot be considered righteous merely because it retains institutions, traditions, or religious language. The quality of its public life is revealed by the way authority is exercised and by whether justice is available to those who need it.8

This emphasis should be read alongside, rather than in place of, the other biblical descriptions of Sodom. Isaiah 1 does not reproduce the details of Genesis 19, and its immediate focus is Judah's worship and injustice. Ezekiel's comparison highlights a related but distinct set of concerns, including pride, abundance, and failure to strengthen the poor and needy. The passages can illuminate one another because both use Sodom's memory to confront later communities, yet each prophet selects language suited to the argument being made. Isaiah's distinctive contribution is the close relationship he establishes between acceptable worship and the practice of justice.

The chapter therefore challenges any reading of faith that separates devotion from responsibility. Isaiah does not permit the people to treat worship as a substitute for justice, nor does he allow religious identity to become protection from moral examination. His call is directed toward a community whose public conduct must become consistent with the God it claims to serve. In this prophetic setting, the memory of Sodom becomes a warning against the presumption that religious privilege can survive indefinitely alongside oppression and corruption.

CHAPTER 17

How Sodom Became a Prophetic Warning

“As [it was] in the overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah and their neighboring cities, says the Lord, no man shall dwell there; neither shall a son of man live in it temporarily.”

Jeremiah 49:18

Isaiah's use of Sodom belongs to a wider prophetic pattern in which the remembered city becomes a language of warning. The prophets do not all use the name in the same way, and the distinction matters. Sometimes Sodom functions as a comparison for moral corruption; elsewhere the name evokes the completeness of a judgment or the desolation associated with a destroyed place. The memory remains recognizable, but its application changes according to the audience and purpose of each prophetic message. Reading these passages together reveals a developing tradition rather than a single fixed formula.9

Isaiah himself uses Sodom in more than one setting. In Isaiah 1, the names of Sodom and Gomorrah are applied to Judah's rulers and people as part of a confrontation over rebellion, worship, and injustice. Isaiah 3:8–9 continues the theme of public wrongdoing and describes the people as having brought disaster upon themselves, with their conduct displayed openly. The comparison with Sodom in that passage reinforces the seriousness of Judah's condition. The prophet's concern remains the moral state of the community and the consequences of a people whose actions testify against them.10

Elsewhere, Isaiah 13:19 uses the overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah as an image for the coming desolation of Babylon. The context is different from Isaiah 1. The prophet is announcing judgment against an imperial power, and the remembered destruction of Sodom supplies a comparison for the completeness of the devastation being described. This passage does not repeat the argument about Judah's worship or the protection of the vulnerable. Instead, it draws on the remembered fate of Sodom to communicate the severity of Babylon's coming downfall. The same historical memory can therefore serve more than one prophetic purpose without losing its connection to the earlier narrative.11

Other prophetic books preserve similar uses. Jeremiah compares the coming judgment of certain nations with the overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah, while Amos invokes the destruction of cities as a sign of divine judgment and yet describes the people's failure to return. These references show that Sodom's memory had become part of a broader prophetic vocabulary for speaking about judgment, accountability, and the consequences of persistent wrongdoing. Each passage must still be read in its own setting, since a comparison involving destruction does not automatically carry every detail or every moral emphasis found in Genesis 19.12

The development of this prophetic language helps explain why later biblical writers could refer to Sodom without retelling the whole story. By the time a prophet used the name, the audience could be expected to recognize the force of the comparison. Yet recognition does not remove the need for interpretation. Readers must still ask whether the passage emphasizes injustice, arrogance, rebellion, desolation, or another aspect of the remembered judgment. The surrounding verses, the historical audience, and the literary purpose provide the boundaries for responsible interpretation.

Seen across the parts studied so far, the biblical memory of Sodom has accumulated several layers. Genesis provides the narrative of the city and its destruction. Ezekiel uses Sodom in a warning to Jerusalem that includes pride, abundance, and neglect of the poor and needy. Leviticus records covenant legislation for Israel, including laws concerning sexual conduct, within its own legal and theological framework. Isaiah uses Sodom to expose the contradiction between religious observance and injustice, and elsewhere draws upon the city's destruction as an image of judgment. These witnesses are related, but they should not be collapsed into one undifferentiated statement about what every passage means.

This distinction prepares the way for the next part of the study. The New Testament refers to Sodom in several different contexts, including teachings of Jesus and writings that use the city as an example of judgment. Those passages deserve the same care given to Genesis, Ezekiel, Leviticus, and Isaiah. The question will not simply be whether the New Testament mentions Sodom, but how each writer uses the memory of the city, what point the passage makes to its first audience, and how those references relate to the larger biblical tradition.

WHAT ISAIAH ADDS TO THE JOURNEY

Isaiah shows how the memory of Sodom could be brought into the public life of a later generation as a warning against religious hypocrisy, corrupt leadership, and the neglect of justice. His opening chapter confronts Judah with the contradiction between continued worship and the mistreatment of vulnerable people, while other prophetic passages use Sodom's remembered destruction to describe the seriousness of coming judgment. The prophet's contribution is therefore both moral and literary: he shows how an earlier story could become a means of examining the conduct of a society that believed itself secure in its religious identity.

With Isaiah, the study has moved from the original narrative, through prophetic remembrance and covenant legislation, into the wider use of Sodom as a warning within Israel's prophetic tradition. The next part turns to Jesus and the New Testament, where Sodom appears again in teachings and writings shaped by their own audiences and purposes. Reading those references in context will allow the study to follow the memory of Sodom into a new period without assuming that every later use repeats the same argument.
