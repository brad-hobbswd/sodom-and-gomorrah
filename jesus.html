PART SIX

SODOM IN THE NEW TESTAMENT

By the first century, Sodom was already a well-known biblical example of judgment. The name carried centuries of memory behind it.

That means the writers of the New Testament could mention Sodom without retelling the entire story. Their audiences already knew the basic tradition, and each writer could draw attention to a particular aspect of its meaning.

Jesus, Peter, and Jude therefore give us another stage in the development of Sodom's historical memory. They do not simply repeat Genesis 19 word for word. They apply the memory of Sodom to the people and circumstances of their own time.

CHAPTER 11

Jesus and Sodom

By the first century, Sodom was already a familiar biblical example of judgment.

Jesus therefore did not need to retell the entire story when He mentioned it.

In Matthew 10, Jesus compares those who reject His messengers with Sodom and Gomorrah. His warning comes as He sends His disciples to proclaim His message and instructs them about how people may receive or reject them.

The comparison involves rejection and judgment.

Jesus was speaking to a Jewish audience that already knew the story of Sodom. The name itself carried meaning. His listeners did not need a complete explanation of Genesis 19 in order to understand why Sodom represented a serious example of divine judgment.

That historical context is important.

Jesus was invoking a familiar example rather than giving a new definition of every sin committed by Sodom.

Matthew 10 therefore should not be turned into a statement that rejection of God's messengers was the only sin Sodom had ever committed. The passage has a particular purpose within Jesus' instructions to His disciples.

It demonstrates how biblical memory could function in the first century. A well-known historical story could be invoked briefly because the audience already understood its larger significance.

CHAPTER 12

Peter and the Destruction of Sodom

Second Peter also looks backward at Sodom.

Peter uses the destruction of the cities as an example of God's judgment against ungodliness. The story becomes evidence that God sees wickedness and that judgment is not outside the biblical understanding of divine justice.

In 2 Peter 2, the discussion includes lawlessness, corrupt desire, and rejection of authority. Peter places the Sodom narrative within a larger series of examples involving people and beings who turned away from what God required.

Peter is therefore doing more than mentioning a location. He is using a remembered historical judgment to make an argument about the moral condition of his own audience.

Once again, Sodom is functioning as a historical example.

Peter is not rewriting Genesis.

He is applying its warning to his own audience.

This is another important development in the biblical tradition. The same event can be remembered by later writers for different purposes without those writers needing to list every detail of the original narrative.

CHAPTER 13

Jude and the Strange Flesh

Jude provides one of the clearest New Testament references to the sexual dimension of the Sodom tradition.

Jude 6 first speaks about angels who did not keep their proper position but abandoned their own dwelling. Then Jude 7 immediately turns to Sodom and Gomorrah and makes a comparison with those angels.

Jude 7 says that Sodom and Gomorrah and the surrounding cities “in like manner” gave themselves over to sexual immorality and went after “strange” or “different” flesh. The verse then presents them as an example of judgment.

This wording matters because Jude 7 does not merely describe Sodom as generally wicked or inhospitable. The verse itself explicitly uses sexual language.

The Greek phrase translated “strange flesh” or “different flesh” is sarkos heteras. Sarkos means “flesh.” Heteras means “different,” “other,” or “another kind.” The Greek text therefore gives us the idea of pursuing flesh that is different or other. citeturn0search1turn0search15

The phrase must also be read in its immediate context. Jude 6 is about angels who abandoned their proper dwelling, and Jude 7 says Sodom acted “in like manner” to these. That close connection is why many interpreters understand Jude to be connecting the sexual wrongdoing of Sodom with the unusual nature of the beings involved in the Genesis tradition. citeturn0search0turn0search12

Genesis 19 describes the visitors as heavenly messengers, while Jude places the Sodom account immediately after his reference to rebellious angels. This creates a significant literary connection. Jude is not simply repeating the Genesis story. He is interpreting it through the tradition available to his audience.

There is also an important limit to what the Greek word establishes.

Heteras means “different” or “other.” It does not itself mean “animal,” “beast,” or “bestial.” Therefore, the specific claim that Jude's phrase means “ontological bestiality” goes beyond the lexical meaning of the Greek words.

The strongest historical conclusion is more precise: Jude 7 explicitly connects Sodom with sexual immorality and the pursuit of “different” or “strange” flesh, and he places that statement directly after his discussion of rebellious angels. The sexual dimension is explicit. The exact meaning of “different flesh” within that comparison remains an interpretive question.

This distinction is important. We should not remove the sexual meaning from Jude 7, but we should also avoid claiming that one particular modern label is the only possible explanation of the ancient Greek wording.

Jude therefore adds an important layer to the historical memory of Sodom. By the time Jude was written, Sodom was being remembered not only as a city judged by God, but also as an example of sexual immorality connected in the text with the unusual “different flesh” and the preceding account of rebellious angels.

WHAT THE NEW TESTAMENT ADDS

The New Testament does not give us one single definition of Sodom. Jesus uses Sodom as an example of rejection and judgment. Peter uses it as an example of divine judgment against ungodliness, lawlessness, corrupt desire, and rebellion. Jude explicitly connects Sodom with sexual immorality and “different” flesh in the context of rebellious angels.

These references demonstrate how the memory of Sodom continued to develop. The original story remained important, but later writers could draw different lessons from it for their own audiences.
