PART SIX

Isaiah's prophetic message brought the study to a point where the memory of Sodom had become a means of examining the life of a later community. Judah's rulers and people were confronted with the contradiction between religious observance and the practice of justice, while other prophetic passages drew upon Sodom's remembered destruction to describe the seriousness of judgment. With that prophetic witness in view, the study now moves into the New Testament, where Sodom appears within the teaching of Jesus and the writings associated with Peter and Jude. These references belong to a later historical period, but they continue to engage a story that had already become deeply established within Israel's Scriptures and religious memory.

By the first century, the account of Sodom was familiar enough that a writer could invoke the city's name without repeating the full narrative of Genesis. The name could call to mind divine judgment, the destruction of a wicked community, and the warning carried by that remembered event. Yet familiarity should not lead readers to assume that every New Testament reference makes the same point. Jesus uses Sodom in warnings directed toward towns and hearers who encounter His message; Second Peter places the destruction within a series of examples concerning judgment and deliverance; and Jude connects Sodom's conduct with sexual immorality and the pursuit of different flesh in a passage that also refers to rebellious angels. Each text must therefore be examined in its own literary setting before the passages are brought into conversation with one another.

This part follows those three witnesses in turn. The purpose is not to force them into a single explanation or to make one passage answer questions raised by another. Instead, the discussion will consider what each writer actually says, the audience being addressed, and the role Sodom plays in the argument. Such an approach preserves the significance of the New Testament references while allowing their differences to remain visible. The result is a clearer account of how the memory of Sodom continued to function within early Christian teaching and interpretation.

CHAPTER 18

Jesus and the Judgment of Sodom

“Truly I tell you, it shall be more tolerable on the day of judgment for the land of Sodom and Gomorrah than for that town.”

Matthew 10:15

Jesus refers to Sodom in several passages, and the setting of each reference helps determine the point being made. In Matthew 10:5–15, He sends the twelve disciples to the people of Israel and gives them instructions concerning their mission. They are to proclaim that the kingdom of heaven has drawn near, minister among the people, and depend upon the hospitality offered to them. Jesus also explains how they should respond when a household or town refuses to receive them or listen to their words. The comparison with Sodom appears within these instructions, where the rejection of the messengers is presented as a matter carrying serious consequences at the day of judgment.1

The force of the comparison rests on the seriousness of rejecting a message that has been delivered with divine authority. Jesus does not retell the events of Genesis 19, nor does He provide a catalogue of the offenses committed by Sodom's inhabitants. Instead, He invokes the city as an established example of judgment and applies that memory to towns that refuse the disciples and their proclamation. The statement that judgment will be more tolerable for Sodom than for an unresponsive town intensifies the warning. The comparison suggests that receiving a message and being accountable for one's response are matters of considerable spiritual weight.

This passage must be read within the mission discourse rather than separated from the instructions that surround it. The disciples are not told to measure the worth of every town by comparing its residents with the people of Sodom. They are being prepared for the possibility that their message will be rejected, and Jesus explains that such rejection does not pass unnoticed. The shaking of dust from their feet becomes a public sign that the messengers have completed their responsibility in that place. The reference to Sodom underscores the seriousness of the response without changing the immediate subject of the passage, which is the reception or rejection of the disciples' proclamation.2

Matthew 11:20–24 develops the comparison in another direction. Jesus rebukes the towns in which many of His mighty works had been performed because they did not repent. Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum are named, and their response is compared with the response that might have been expected from Tyre, Sidon, and Sodom under different circumstances. Jesus declares that the judgment will be more bearable for Sodom than for Capernaum. The issue in this passage is not simply that the towns have heard a message and refused hospitality to traveling disciples. They have witnessed works associated with Jesus' ministry and have nevertheless failed to respond with repentance.3

The comparison with Sodom is especially striking because the city is already remembered as an example of severe judgment. Jesus' words do not minimize the wrongdoing associated with Sodom or suggest that its destruction was insignificant. Rather, the comparison emphasizes the accountability of those who have received greater opportunity to recognize God's activity and yet remain unresponsive. Capernaum's privileged position does not provide protection from judgment; in the logic of the passage, greater exposure to the works of Jesus brings greater responsibility to respond. The warning therefore concerns the relationship between revelation, repentance, and accountability.

Luke preserves parallel material in Luke 10:1–16, where Jesus sends a wider group of disciples ahead of Him. Their mission instructions include a warning for towns that refuse to receive them, and the comparison with Sodom again emphasizes the seriousness of rejection. Luke 10:12–15 also places the warning beside the comparison involving Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum. The parallel passages show that the memory of Sodom was an established part of the teaching tradition concerning judgment, while each Gospel situates the material within its own presentation of Jesus' mission and the response to His ministry.4

Jesus refers to Sodom again in Luke 17:28–32, where the days of Lot become an illustration within a teaching about the coming of the Son of Man. The people in Lot's time are described as eating, drinking, buying, selling, planting, and building until the day Lot left Sodom, when fire and brimstone brought destruction. The ordinary activities named in the passage are not condemned as inherently sinful. They portray a population continuing its routines without recognizing the approaching crisis. Jesus uses the suddenness of the destruction to warn His hearers against becoming so absorbed in ordinary life that they are unprepared for the decisive moment of judgment.5

The reference to Lot's wife strengthens the warning. Jesus tells His hearers to remember her, connecting the story with the danger of turning back when deliverance requires decisive departure. The surrounding teaching concerns readiness, attachment, loss, and the unexpected arrival of the day of the Son of Man. The comparison with Noah's generation earlier in Luke 17 reinforces the same theme: people continue with ordinary life until judgment arrives. The passage therefore draws upon the remembered events surrounding Lot to teach vigilance and readiness, not to provide a comprehensive account of every reason for Sodom's destruction.

Across these passages, Jesus uses Sodom in related but distinguishable ways. In Matthew 10 and Luke 10, the city provides a comparison for towns that reject the disciples' message. In Matthew 11 and Luke 10, Sodom appears in a warning about the responsibility of communities that witness the works of Jesus and do not repent. In Luke 17, the days of Lot illustrate the suddenness of judgment and the need for readiness. These uses share a serious view of accountability, yet each passage directs the memory of Sodom toward a particular aspect of Jesus' teaching. Reading them carefully allows the warning to retain its force without making the Gospel references say more than their contexts establish.

CHAPTER 19

Peter and the Destruction of Sodom

“And He condemned to ruin and extinction the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah, reducing them to ashes [and thus] set them forth as an example to those who would be ungodly;”

2 Peter 2:6

Second Peter refers to Sodom within a larger argument about false teachers, destructive conduct, and the certainty of divine judgment. The chapter opens by warning that false teachers will arise among the people and introduce destructive teachings. Their conduct is described through images of exploitation, greed, sensuality, and disregard for the truth. The writer then turns to examples from the past to support the argument that wrongdoing does not escape divine notice. The flood generation, the judgment of the angels who sinned, and the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah are brought together as examples of judgment, while the rescue of Noah and Lot demonstrates that deliverance is also part of the account.6

The structure of the argument is important because the writer does not mention Sodom as an isolated illustration. The account belongs to a sequence in which judgment and rescue appear together. Second Peter 2:6 describes the cities being reduced to ashes and made an example for those who would live ungodly lives afterward. The following verses identify Lot as a righteous man distressed by the lawless conduct he observed. The passage therefore uses the destruction of the cities to affirm that persistent ungodliness is subject to judgment, while Lot's rescue demonstrates that the Lord is able to deliver the godly from trial.7

The description of Lot deserves careful attention. Genesis portrays him as a complicated figure whose decisions and actions invite discussion, and later biblical writers do not necessarily emphasize every feature of his narrative. Second Peter focuses on Lot's distress over the lawless behavior around him. The writer's purpose is not to provide a complete character study or to resolve every interpretive question raised by Genesis 19. Instead, Lot functions within the argument as an example of someone living amid conduct that grieved him and from which God delivered him. The distinction between the Genesis narrative and the later use of Lot is important for understanding how biblical writers select and apply earlier material.

Second Peter also describes the conduct of the people of Sodom and Gomorrah as involving lawlessness. The passage's wider vocabulary includes sensuality, corrupt desire, and contempt for authority, particularly in its description of the false teachers being opposed. The comparison draws the reader's attention to a pattern of ungodly conduct and to the certainty of judgment. The writer does not pause to repeat the details of the confrontation at Lot's house, and the reference should not be made to carry a detailed description that the passage itself does not supply. Its force lies in the use of Sodom's destruction as a warning within the letter's argument about false teaching and immoral behavior.8

The language of example is central to the passage. Sodom's destruction is not recalled merely as an event from the distant past; it is presented as a warning for later readers. The memory of judgment gives urgency to the writer's appeal for discernment and faithful conduct. The example is intended to shape how the audience understands the claims of those who teach and the consequences of lives characterized by ungodliness. In this respect, Second Peter continues a pattern already visible in the prophets and in the teaching of Jesus: the remembered fate of Sodom becomes a means of addressing the responsibilities of a later community.

The relationship between judgment and deliverance also prevents the passage from being reduced to a warning alone. Second Peter 2:9 draws the argument together by stating that the Lord knows how to deliver the godly from trials and to keep the unrighteous under punishment for the day of judgment. The writer's concern is not simply to frighten the audience with a remembered catastrophe. The examples are intended to establish confidence that God recognizes both the conduct of the ungodly and the circumstances of those who seek to remain faithful. Judgment and rescue are presented as complementary dimensions of divine justice.9

There are important questions concerning the relationship between Second Peter and the letter of Jude, since the two writings share language, examples, and themes. Scholars have proposed different explanations for the literary relationship between them, including the possibility that one writer used the other or that both drew upon shared traditions. The precise direction of dependence remains debated, and the existence of parallels should not be treated as proof of a conclusion that the texts themselves do not explicitly state. For the present study, the essential point is that both writings use remembered examples of rebellion and judgment to confront conduct considered destructive within their communities.10

Second Peter therefore adds a distinctive witness to the New Testament memory of Sodom. The cities are remembered as an example of judgment against ungodliness, while Lot's deliverance supports the assurance that God knows how to rescue those who are faithful amid corrupt surroundings. The passage applies the ancient account to the problem of false teaching and destructive conduct in the writer's own setting. Its message is serious, but its argument is broader than a single category of wrongdoing: it concerns the character of those who lead others astray, the reality of divine judgment, and the possibility of deliverance.

CHAPTER 20

Jude and the Warning of Different Flesh

“[The wicked are sentenced to suffer] just as Sodom and Gomorrah and the adjacent towns—which likewise gave themselves over to impurity and indulged in unnatural vice and sensual perversity—are laid out [in plain sight] as an exhibit of perpetual punishment [to warn] of everlasting fire.”

Jude 1:7

The letter of Jude offers one of the most concentrated New Testament reflections on Sodom. Its opening appeal urges the readers to contend for the faith and warns that certain people have entered the community and are turning grace into an excuse for immoral conduct while rejecting rightful authority. Jude supports his warning by recalling examples of rebellion and judgment from Israel's Scriptures and from traditions familiar to his audience. Within that sequence, he refers to the angels who did not keep their proper position, the people of Sodom and Gomorrah, and other examples that illustrate the danger of rejecting the boundaries established by God. The passage is brief, but its arrangement and wording require close attention.11

Jude 6 describes angels who did not keep their own domain but abandoned their proper dwelling, and who are kept under restraint for judgment. Jude 7 then turns to Sodom, Gomorrah, and the surrounding cities, describing them as having acted in a similar manner, giving themselves over to sexual immorality and pursuing different flesh. The close placement of these verses has led interpreters to consider how Jude connects the conduct of the cities with the preceding account of the angels. The wording clearly places sexual wrongdoing within Jude's description of Sodom, while the precise force of the comparison with the angels requires attention to the language and traditions behind the passage.12

The expression commonly translated “strange flesh” in the King James Version is rendered in many modern translations as “other flesh” or “different flesh.” The Greek phrase is sarkos heteras, in which sarkos is a form of the word for flesh and heteras conveys the sense of other or different. The phrase is significant because Jude does not merely say that the people pursued sexual immorality; he adds language that marks the flesh as different. However, the vocabulary alone does not settle every question about the precise referent. The meaning must be considered alongside the reference to the angels in verse 6, the Genesis account, and the interpretive traditions available in the ancient world.13

Genesis 19 describes the men of Sodom surrounding Lot's house and demanding access to the visitors who had entered his home. Lot's visitors are identified in the narrative as messengers, and the threatened act is one of sexual violence directed against those guests. Jude's language is not a quotation of the Genesis narrative, and his brief summary does not repeat every detail of the scene. Nevertheless, the relationship between the passages is important: Jude interprets the remembered conduct of Sodom through the language of sexual immorality and the pursuit of different flesh. His wording gives an explicitly sexual dimension to the New Testament memory of the city, even as the exact scope of the phrase remains a matter for careful interpretation.

The reference to the angels in Jude 6 also reflects a wider interpretive tradition concerning the rebellion of heavenly beings. Ancient Jewish writings developed the brief account of the “sons of God” in Genesis 6:1–4 into more extended narratives about angels who crossed an appointed boundary. The Book of 1 Enoch is an important example of this tradition, describing the Watchers and their transgression. Jude's audience may have recognized this interpretive background, although the precise sources and extent of Jude's dependence are debated. The presence of such traditions helps explain why Jude's sequence links angelic rebellion and the conduct of Sodom, but responsible interpretation should distinguish the biblical text from later expansions of its narrative.14

Several interpretations have been offered for the phrase “different flesh.” Some readers understand the phrase primarily through the attempted assault on the visitors in Genesis 19, emphasizing the pursuit of beings who were not ordinary human guests. Others place greater weight on the sexual violence and boundary violation in the Sodom narrative without making the visitors' heavenly identity the exclusive focus. The relationship between Jude 6 and 7 is central to this discussion, and interpreters differ over whether “in like manner” most directly compares the cities with the angels, the surrounding cities with Sodom, or the sequence of transgressions more broadly. The wording invites comparison, but the precise grammatical and conceptual relationship should not be treated as though no interpretive question exists.15

What can be stated with confidence is that Jude presents Sodom and the surrounding cities as an example of sexual immorality and pursuit of different flesh, and that he places this example immediately after his warning about angels who abandoned their proper dwelling. Jude also presents the cities as an example of judgment. These observations are substantial and should not be minimized. At the same time, the Greek adjective translated “different” does not by itself mean “animal,” “beast,” or “bestial,” and the phrase should not be made to prove a technical category that its vocabulary does not express. The strongest reading begins with what Jude says and then explains, with appropriate qualification, the interpretive questions that remain.

Jude's reference to “eternal fire” also belongs to the letter's language of judgment. The expression recalls the severity of Sodom's destruction and places the city within a warning about the consequences of rebellion. The image should be read within Jude's argument rather than detached as a complete description of every detail of the afterlife. Jude is addressing people whose conduct threatens the integrity of the community, and his examples are intended to make clear that privilege, status, or confident claims do not protect anyone who persists in rejecting the boundaries and authority of God.16

Jude therefore contributes a particularly important stage in the New Testament use of Sodom. Unlike the references in Jesus' teaching that emphasize rejection, repentance, readiness, and judgment, Jude explicitly includes sexual immorality in his description of the cities. His wording also places the account beside a tradition about rebellious angels and the violation of an appointed boundary. The passage must be allowed to retain both features: its direct language about sexual wrongdoing and its literary connection with the preceding example. Careful interpretation neither removes the sexual dimension nor claims that the Greek phrase alone answers every question about the nature of the transgression.

WHAT THE NEW TESTAMENT ADDS

The New Testament preserves several distinct uses of Sodom's memory. Jesus invokes the city when warning towns that reject His messengers, communities that witness His works without repentance, and hearers who must remain ready for the coming judgment. Second Peter remembers the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah as an example of judgment against ungodliness while also emphasizing Lot's deliverance. Jude explicitly associates the cities with sexual immorality and the pursuit of different flesh, placing that account beside his reference to rebellious angels. These passages share a serious concern with accountability, but each writer shapes the memory of Sodom to serve a particular argument.

The study has now followed Sodom from the Genesis narrative through prophetic remembrance, covenant legislation, and New Testament interpretation. The next part turns to David and Jonathan, a different biblical subject that requires its own careful reading of narrative, language, historical setting, and later interpretation. The transition is not a claim that the stories are identical or directly connected; rather, the study will continue examining how biblical relationships and later assumptions must be evaluated through the evidence of the texts themselves.
